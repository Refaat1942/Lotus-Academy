## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish room-temperature storage from refrigerated storage using the product leaflet and pack label
2. Organise a pharmacy fridge correctly and keep it free from mistakes that damage products
3. Monitor and record temperatures on a regular schedule
4. Recognise a temperature excursion and respond with safe general principles
5. Act correctly when a fridge fails, escalating to the pharmacist and the manager and following the Lotus SOP

---

## 1. Why storage conditions matter

Medicines and many health products are sensitive to heat, light, humidity and freezing. Egypt has long hot summers, and a branch can become hot inside, especially near windows, storerooms without ventilation or during power cuts. A product stored badly can lose its quality without any visible sign. The customer cannot see the damage, so the pharmacy is responsible for protecting the product from the moment it is received until the moment it is handed over.

The golden rule: **the product leaflet and pack label are the first reference for storage, and the Lotus SOP defines how we apply it.** If a product says "store in a refrigerator" or "do not store above" a stated temperature, that instruction wins over habit or guesswork.

## 2. Room temperature versus refrigerated products

| Feature | Room-temperature storage | Refrigerated storage |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Shelves and drawers away from heat, direct sun and moisture | A dedicated pharmacy fridge |
| Instruction on the pack | A "do not store above" temperature, "store in a cool, dry place", "protect from light" | "Store in a refrigerator", commonly a range such as 2 to 8 degrees Celsius, as written on the leaflet |
| Examples | Many tablets, capsules, syrups, dermocosmetics, baby products | Some insulin products, some eye drops, some vaccines and biological products, some suppositories and creams |
| Main risks | Hot rooms, sunlight, humidity, bathrooms and kitchens, window displays | Door left open, overloading, freezing against the back wall, power failure |

The examples above are general; always check each product's leaflet. Some products can be stored at room temperature before opening and in the fridge after opening, or the reverse. Read the label, not the memory.

**Important**: "Room temperature" in the leaflet is a defined condition, and a hot Egyptian summer afternoon inside a closed storeroom may exceed it. If the branch gets too hot, tell the manager; air conditioning and ventilation are part of storage control.

## 3. The pharmacy fridge: correct use

A pharmacy fridge is for **medicines and health products only**.

**Do**
- Use a dedicated fridge, with a thermometer inside that you can read without opening the door for long.
- Store products on shelves with space for air to move.
- Keep products in their original boxes.
- Put products with the earliest expiry at the front (FEFO).
- Label the fridge with the responsible role and the daily check sheet.
- Move deliveries of refrigerated items into the fridge immediately.

**Do not**
- Store food, drinks or staff lunch in the pharmacy fridge.
- Place products against the back wall where they may freeze, or in the door, where temperature changes most, unless the SOP states otherwise.
- Overload the fridge so air cannot circulate.
- Open the door repeatedly or leave it open while organising.
- Plug the fridge into an overloaded extension that might be switched off.

## 4. Temperature monitoring and logging

Monitoring proves that the product has been kept correctly. If it is not recorded, it cannot be proven.

**Daily routine**
1. Read the thermometer at the times set by the Lotus SOP (usually at least at opening and at closing, and more often if the SOP says so).
2. Record the current temperature and, where the thermometer shows them, the minimum and maximum since the last reading.
3. Record the date, time and the initials or role of the person who checked. Use role or employee code as the SOP states.
4. Compare the reading with the acceptable range on the log.
5. If the reading is outside the range, do not just write it down: start the excursion procedure at once.
6. Reset the min/max thermometer after reading, if it has that function.
7. The manager reviews and signs the logs on the schedule set by the SOP.

**Example log (illustrative layout)**

| Date | Time | Current | Min | Max | In range? | Action | Checked by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | Opening | | | | Yes / No | None / see note | Role |
| Entry | Closing | | | | Yes / No | None / see note | Role |

Never fill the log from memory or "pre-fill" it. A false record is worse than a missing record because it hides a real risk.

Also have a thermometer for the room where room-temperature stock is kept, and record it if the SOP requires it, especially in hot months.

## 5. Temperature excursions

A **temperature excursion** is when a product has been outside its stated storage conditions, even for a short time.

Common causes: a power cut, a door left ajar, a faulty thermostat, a new delivery placed against the wrong wall, an overloaded fridge, a hot room, or a delivery that travelled for a long time without proper packaging.

What to do in general terms:

1. Do not sell or dispense the affected products until a decision is made. Mark them clearly as "do not use" and separate them.
2. Record what happened: the reading, the time, how long, which products.
3. Tell the pharmacist in charge and the pharmacy manager immediately.
4. The pharmacist checks the leaflet for the product's guidance, and, where the leaflet is unclear, contacts the manufacturer or distributor through the channel in the SOP. Do not decide alone by "it looks fine".
5. Follow the decision: continue using the product only if it is confirmed safe; otherwise quarantine and dispose of it according to the SOP and current official rules.
6. Record the outcome and the corrective action.

Never refreeze, re-chill or "test" a product to see if it still works, and never tell a customer that a product is "probably fine" if you are not sure.

## 6. When a fridge fails

Fridge failure may mean a power cut, a broken compressor or a tripped plug. The first minutes matter.

**General first-response steps**
1. **Keep the door closed.** A closed fridge holds its temperature longer. Do not open it to look.
2. Check the simple causes quickly: plug, switch, circuit, power in the whole branch.
3. Inform the pharmacist in charge and the pharmacy manager at once, and note the time the problem was found and the last known good reading.
4. Prepare the backup plan from the SOP: a second working fridge, a validated cool box with cooling packs, or transfer to another Lotus branch. Use only what the SOP approves.
5. Before moving products, read the current temperature, and record it. Transfer products carefully, keeping them in original boxes, and monitor the temperature in the backup storage.
6. Call maintenance or the service contact according to the SOP.
7. Mark all affected products and keep them separate until the pharmacist decides, following the leaflet and Lotus SOP.
8. After repair, check the fridge holds its range before returning products, and record everything.

Be especially careful with high-value or sensitive products such as insulin, vaccines and biological products. The leaflet and the SOP decide, not the team's guess.

**Customer communication**: if a customer is waiting for a refrigerated product during a failure, the pharmacist explains calmly that the product needs to be confirmed safe and offers an alternative path (another branch, later delivery). Never hand over a product with uncertain storage.

## 7. Delivery, home delivery and carrying to the customer

Refrigerated products leaving the pharmacy need care too. When a customer takes insulin or another refrigerated product home, the pharmacist gives general advice from the leaflet about keeping it cool during the trip. For delivery by a courier, use packaging approved by the SOP and limit the time between dispatch and delivery.

## Scenario

On a hot afternoon in a branch in Alexandria, the power goes off in the whole street at around the busiest time. The pharmacy fridge holds insulin pens, eye drops and some creams. The technician on shift sees the fridge light is off.

**Model approach**
1. Keep the fridge closed; tell the pharmacist and the manager at once.
2. Record the time and read the thermometer (current, min and max) without holding the door open.
3. Check whether the power returns soon and whether the building has a backup source. If the SOP allows, prepare the approved cool box.
4. If the power does not return within the time set by the SOP, move products to the approved backup storage, keeping a temperature record.
5. When power returns, check the fridge temperature stays within range, and let the pharmacist decide, using the leaflet and SOP, which products can continue to be used and which must be quarantined.
6. Record the incident, the decisions and the corrective actions (for example, an unplugged-alarm or a backup plan).

## Key takeaways

- The leaflet and pack label are the first reference for storage; the SOP defines how we apply it.
- A pharmacy fridge holds medicines and health products only, with space for air and products in original boxes.
- Temperatures are read and recorded on schedule, never pre-filled or invented.
- Any reading outside the range starts the excursion procedure: separate, record, inform, decide.
- When a fridge fails, keep the door closed, inform, follow the backup plan and let the pharmacist decide on the products.
- Never sell a product whose storage history is uncertain.
