## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Give a structured shift handover that leaves nothing important unsaid
2. Communicate clearly within the team during busy periods and when asking for help
3. Protect patient privacy and confidentiality at the counter, on the phone and in messages
4. Keep professional boundaries with customers and colleagues
5. Give and receive feedback constructively

---

## 1. Why internal communication matters

A pharmacy works as a chain. A customer's experience depends not only on the pharmacist at the counter but also on the technician who prepared the order, the colleague who counted the stock and the previous shift who promised a call-back. Most serious errors in busy workplaces happen at hand-offs, when information is lost between people. Good internal communication protects customers and protects colleagues.

## 2. Shift handover

At the end of a shift, the outgoing pharmacist passes information to the incoming one. The handover should be short, structured and, where the SOP requires, written.

### A simple structure: SBAR-style

| Step | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | What is happening now | "A customer is waiting for a call-back about an unavailable item." |
| Background | Relevant history | "She asked this morning, we promised an answer by evening." |
| Action done | What has already been done | "I checked two branches, one will have it tomorrow." |
| Request | What the next person must do | "Please call her at five and confirm." |

### What to hand over

- Pending customer promises: call-backs, reserved items, delivery issues.
- Stock problems: shortages, near-expiry items, damaged goods, cold-chain concerns.
- Safety issues: a dispensing near-miss, a customer complaint, a product concern.
- Equipment or cash issues according to the SOP.
- Instructions from the manager.

### Handover rules

- Do it in person where possible, in a quiet place, away from customers.
- Use the branch notebook or the approved system for written items; do not rely on memory.
- The receiving person repeats back the key points, which is teach-back among colleagues.
- Never leave a handover incomplete because of a rush.

> **Outgoing pharmacist:** Three things: the call-back for the unavailable item at five, the box of syrup in the fridge that is near expiry and marked, and a customer complaint about a delay that the manager already knows about.
> **Incoming pharmacist:** So: call-back at five, check the marked syrup in the fridge, and the delay complaint is already with the manager. Anything else?

## 3. Communicating within the team

- **Be direct and respectful.** "Please can you take the next customer?" is better than hints or sighs.
- **Use short, closed-loop messages.** When you give an instruction, the other person repeats it, and then confirms when done.
- **Call out clearly in a busy branch**, for example "Pharmacist needed at the counter" rather than shouting a customer's details across the room.
- **Ask for help early.** A pharmacist who is unsure of an interaction or a dose asks a colleague or checks a reference before dispensing.
- **Share information, not blame.** After a mistake or near-miss, discuss what happened and how to prevent it.
- **Use the right channel.** A team WhatsApp group is for operational information and must not contain customer-identifying health data unless the SOP specifically allows it.
- **Respect roles.** Pharmacists, technicians and sales staff each have a scope; communicate when something is outside your scope and needs a pharmacist.

## 4. Confidentiality and privacy

Patients share sensitive information: illnesses, family planning, mental health, skin problems, sexual health. Trust depends on handling it with discretion. Follow the current rules and the Lotus SOP on patient data.

### At the counter

- Lower your voice for sensitive topics and move to a quieter area when possible.
- Ask private questions discreetly, for example by writing the question on a note, or gesturing to a quieter spot.
- Do not read out the name of a medicine in a way that exposes the condition to others in the queue.
- Do not leave prescriptions, labels or screens visible to other customers.

### On the phone and messaging

- Confirm you are speaking to the right person before sharing details.
- Do not give details of one family member to another unless the customer agreed or the SOP allows it.
- Do not share prescriptions or photos outside the Lotus process.

### With people outside

- Do not discuss customers with friends, family or on social media, even without names. A story may still be recognisable.
- Do not confirm or deny that someone is a customer to a stranger.
- If a family member asks about another person's medicines, explain kindly that you must protect privacy and invite the patient to ask directly.

## 5. Professional boundaries

- **With customers:** be warm but professional. Do not accept personal favours, do not share personal contact details, do not give your private WhatsApp number, and do not give medicines or treatment advice to friends and family that you would not give in the pharmacy.
- **No dispensing outside the rules** for anyone, however well known to you.
- **Gifts and tips:** follow the Lotus policy.
- **With colleagues:** avoid gossip, discuss problems with the person concerned or the manager, and keep conversations about customers professional.
- **Social media:** do not post photos of customers, prescriptions or the dispensing area.

## 6. Giving and receiving feedback

Feedback is information that helps someone improve. It is not criticism of the person.

### Giving feedback

1. **Choose a private moment** and ask permission: "May I share an observation about this morning?"
2. **Be specific and factual:** "When the customer asked about dosage, the explanation was fast and he looked unsure" instead of "You are bad at counselling".
3. **Describe the effect:** "He left without confirming the dose."
4. **Suggest what could help:** "Next time try asking him to repeat how he will take it."
5. **Acknowledge what went well** and finish with support.

### Receiving feedback

- Listen to the end without defending yourself.
- Ask for an example if you do not understand.
- Say thank you, even if you disagree; you can reflect later.
- Choose one action to try and check back later.

> **Colleague:** May I share one observation about the last customer? You explained the dosage clearly, and I noticed the customer did not repeat it back. Maybe you can try asking him to explain how he will take it.
> **Pharmacist:** Thank you, that is helpful. I will try it with the next customer.

## 7. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Leaving the handover to memory | Structured, written, repeated back |
| Shouting a customer's medicines across the pharmacy | Lower voice, use a note, move aside |
| Discussing customers in the staff group with names | Share only what is operationally necessary |
| Giving feedback in front of customers | Private and specific |
| Defending yourself instantly | Listen, thank, then reflect |
| Giving friends a medicine without the normal process | Same rules for everyone |

## Checklist: end of shift

- Pending call-backs and reserved items handed over.
- Stock and expiry concerns reported.
- Safety events and complaints shared with the manager and the next shift.
- Written notes are clear, factual and free of private details beyond what is needed.
- The incoming colleague repeated back the key points.

## Scenario

At the end of a busy Thursday shift, the outgoing pharmacist is tired and wants to leave quickly. She remembers that a customer was promised a call-back about an item expected in the evening, and that a delivery was delayed with the customer upset. She takes two minutes with the incoming pharmacist in the back area: situation, background, what she did, and what is needed. The incoming pharmacist repeats back the points. Later, a customer's relative calls and asks, "What medicines does my father take?" The pharmacist explains kindly, "I am sorry, I cannot share that over the phone. Please ask him to speak with us directly or visit." The next morning, the manager gives brief, private feedback to the technician about speaking too loudly at the counter, with a specific example and a practical suggestion.

## Key takeaways

- Most serious errors happen at hand-offs, so handover must be structured, preferably written and repeated back.
- Hand over pending promises, stock problems, safety events and manager instructions.
- Communicate directly and respectfully in the team, ask for help early and share information, not blame.
- Protect confidentiality at the counter, on the phone, in messaging and outside work.
- Keep professional boundaries with customers and colleagues; the same rules apply to everyone.
- Give feedback privately, specifically and kindly, and receive it by listening and thanking.
