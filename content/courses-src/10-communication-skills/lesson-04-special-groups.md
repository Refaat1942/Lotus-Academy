## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Adapt pace, wording and tools when counselling older adults
2. Communicate with parents and children, including safety messages about dosing and storage
3. Counsel pregnant and breastfeeding women with care and appropriate referral
4. Support people with low health literacy and people with hearing or vision difficulties
5. Involve caregivers respectfully while protecting the patient's dignity and privacy

---

## 1. One approach does not fit everyone

The same counselling talk can work very well for one customer and fail completely for another. Age, language level, hearing, sight, stress and role (patient or caregiver) all change what works. A good pharmacist makes a quick, respectful assessment in the first seconds and then adjusts. The principles of earlier lessons remain: listen, use plain language, check understanding, write it down. This lesson shows how to adapt them.

## 2. Older adults

Many older customers take several medicines and may have reduced hearing, sight or memory. They deserve respect and patience, never impatience or a childish tone.

- **Address them respectfully** and speak to the customer, not only to the companion.
- **Slow down** and use short sentences. Do not shout; lower your pitch and face the person so they can see your lips.
- **Focus on the essentials**: what each medicine is for, when to take it, the main warning.
- **Use a medicine schedule**: a simple written table with large letters, grouped by time of day.
- **Ask about all medicines**, including those from other doctors, herbs and supplements, and mention to the prescriber or ask the customer to show the list to the doctor.
- **Check practical difficulties**: can they open the packaging, read the label, split tablets, remember the times? Suggest practical aids following the SOP, such as a weekly pill organiser.
- **Teach-back** with patience; give time to answer.

> **Pharmacist:** I would like to write the times for you so it is easy at home. This one is for the pressure, in the morning. This one is at night. Could you tell me which one you take in the morning, to be sure I wrote it clearly?

## 3. Parents and children

Parents are often worried and short of sleep. The pharmacist's tasks are to calm, to inform and to protect.

- **Ask the child's age and weight** when relevant, and never advise a dose from memory; follow the leaflet, the prescription and the SOP.
- **Speak to the parent as the partner**, and where age-appropriate speak to the child too: "This will help your tummy feel better."
- **Do not promise instant results** or claim a medicine is "completely safe".
- **Demonstrate measuring**: use the syringe or cup and show the line.
- **Safety messages**: keep all medicines locked or out of reach, in the original packaging, never describe medicines to a child as sweets, and check the expiry of opened syrups according to the leaflet.
- **Fever and young infants**: a young infant with fever, a child who is very drowsy, has trouble breathing or refuses fluids needs urgent medical review, not a product.
- **Be non-judgmental.** Avoid blaming language such as "you should not have waited".

## 4. Pregnant and breastfeeding women

Many women are anxious about any medicine during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and often come to the pharmacy first.

- **Ask privately**, at a quieter end of the counter, whether the customer is pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding, and ask kindly without assuming.
- **Be careful and conservative.** Do not make statements that a medicine is "safe in pregnancy" from memory. Check a reliable reference and the SOP, and refer to the doctor when there is doubt.
- **Acknowledge worry**: "It is good that you asked before taking anything."
- **Encourage contact with the doctor** for ongoing symptoms, new symptoms in pregnancy, or before using any new medicine, herbal product or supplement.
- **Avoid fear**: do not frighten, and do not falsely reassure.

> **Customer:** I have a headache, can I take what I used to take?
> **Pharmacist:** Thank you for asking first. May I ask, are you pregnant or breastfeeding? I will check carefully what is suitable, and if there is any doubt I will advise you to ask your doctor before taking anything.

## 5. People with low health literacy

Low health literacy can affect anyone, including educated people who are stressed or new to a condition. Many customers will not admit they struggle to read.

- **Do not rely on the leaflet.** Explain verbally and demonstrate.
- **Use pictures and symbols** where the branch supports them, and short real-life examples: "After breakfast, with the tea."
- **Break information into small steps** and teach-back each step.
- **Avoid asking "Can you read this?"**; instead say "Let me write it clearly for you."
- **Be patient and never show surprise** at a misunderstanding.
- **Ask open questions** such as "How do you usually take it?" to detect mistakes gently.

## 6. Hearing or vision difficulties

| Situation | What helps |
|---|---|
| Hearing difficulty | Face the customer, reduce noise, speak clearly at a normal volume, use writing, confirm key words by writing numbers |
| Vision difficulty | Describe what you are handing over, read the label aloud, use large print, tactile markers if the SOP allows, suggest asking the family to help with the schedule |
| Both | Allow more time, use written large print and ask permission to guide by touch |

Do not shout at a person with hearing loss. Do not assume that someone who cannot hear well cannot understand. Speak directly to the customer, not to the companion.

## 7. Caregivers

Caregivers (family members, domestic helpers, nurses) often collect and give medicines.

- **Confirm the caregiver's role**: "Who will give the medicine at home?" Counsel that person.
- **Respect the patient's privacy.** Share only what the caregiver needs, following the SOP, and do not discuss sensitive matters in front of others.
- **Give the schedule in writing** to the caregiver and, where possible, advise a single person to be responsible.
- **Recognise caregiver stress.** A kind comment such as "You are doing a lot, thank you for taking care" builds cooperation.
- **Advise on when to call the doctor** and how to keep medicines safely stored.

## 8. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Talking louder and faster to an older person | Slower, clear, facing them |
| Talking only to the companion | Include the customer |
| Using a childish tone | Adult respect |
| Giving doses for children from memory | Check the leaflet and the SOP |
| Saying a medicine is "safe in pregnancy" without checking | Check and refer when in doubt |
| Asking "Can you read?" | Offer to write it clearly |

## Checklist: adapting my approach

- Who is the patient, and who will give or take the medicine?
- Did I check age, pregnancy or breastfeeding status where relevant?
- Is my pace, volume and wording suitable?
- Did I demonstrate and use teach-back?
- Did I write the schedule down?
- Did I protect privacy and refer to the doctor when needed?

## Scenario

An older customer comes with his grandson to collect five medicines. The grandson answers every question. The pharmacist greets the older customer first, faces him, and speaks slowly. "I want to be sure you are comfortable with each medicine. May I write the times for you?" She writes a small table: morning, afternoon, night. Then she asks, "Which ones will you take in the morning?" He points to two boxes. One is wrong. She says, "Thank you, my explanation was not clear enough, let me show you again", and marks the boxes with large letters. She also invites the grandson to keep a copy of the table, and advises them to show the full list to the doctor at the next visit. The customer leaves with a plan he can follow, and the grandson understood that the patient is the person who matters.

## Key takeaways

- Adapt your pace, wording and tools to the person in front of you; the principles stay the same.
- With older adults, slow down, face them, write a clear schedule and ask about all medicines.
- With parents, check age and weight, demonstrate measuring and give clear safety and storage messages.
- With pregnant and breastfeeding women, be conservative, ask privately, check references and refer when in doubt.
- Support low health literacy with demonstration, small steps and kind teach-back.
- Include the customer even when a caregiver is present, and protect privacy.
