## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Distinguish the levels of listening and recognise which level you are using during a busy shift
2. Use open questions and reflecting to uncover the real need behind a customer's request
3. Express empathy in short, sincere statements that fit the Egyptian counter
4. Avoid interrupting and manage waiting customers in a crowded branch
5. Recognise when what you hear is a red flag that needs a doctor, not a product

---

## 1. Why listening is a clinical skill

A customer who says "I want something for my stomach" may mean heartburn, diarrhoea, cramps, a child's colic or a repeat of a prescription they cannot remember. If the pharmacist answers the first words and reaches for a product, the risk is a wrong or unnecessary sale, a missed warning sign or a customer who never returns. Listening is how the pharmacist collects the information needed to counsel safely. It is part of the clinical work, not a courtesy added to it.

In a Lotus branch the pressure is real: queues, a ringing phone, a delivery rider waiting. The skill is to listen well in 30 to 60 seconds, not to listen for ten minutes.

## 2. Levels of listening

| Level | What it looks like | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Pretend listening | Nodding while looking at the screen or the shelf | Customer feels ignored and repeats everything |
| Selective listening | Hearing only the product name or the first symptom | Missing allergy, pregnancy, other medicines |
| Attentive listening | Following the words and facts | Good, but may miss feelings and worry |
| Active, empathic listening | Following facts and feelings, then checking what you understood | Best level for counselling |

Aim for the last level whenever the request involves symptoms, children, chronic illness or a customer who looks worried. For a simple repeat purchase of a known item, attentive listening is enough.

## 3. The techniques of active listening

1. **Stop and face the customer.** Put down the pen or the phone, turn your body towards them and make brief eye contact.
2. **Start with an open question.** "Tell me what is bothering you" gets more than "Is it your stomach?"
3. **Let the first answer finish.** Most people need about 20 to 30 seconds. Do not complete their sentence.
4. **Reflect.** Repeat the main point in your own words: "So the cough started three days ago and it is worse at night, is that right?"
5. **Ask focused questions.** Who is the medicine for, how long, any other medicines, any allergy, pregnancy or breastfeeding?
6. **Summarise before advising.** One sentence that proves you heard: "You need something for a dry night cough for your daughter, and she has no allergies."

### Reflecting, three simple forms

- **Repeating a key word:** Customer: "The pain is sharp." Pharmacist: "Sharp?"
- **Paraphrasing:** "You are saying the tablets make you sleepy during work."
- **Naming the feeling:** "It sounds like you are worried about giving her another dose."

## 4. Empathy in a few words

Empathy means showing that you understand how the customer feels, without pretending to feel the same and without promising a result you cannot guarantee. It is different from sympathy ("poor you") and from fixing ("take this").

Useful short statements:

- "I understand this has been tiring for you."
- "It is natural to worry when a child has a fever at night."
- "Thank you for telling me, this helps me choose the safest option."
- "Let us see what we can do together."

Avoid empty phrases such as "Don't worry, it is nothing" because you cannot know that. Say instead: "Let us check a few things to make sure it is something simple."

### Model dialogue

> **Customer:** I need something strong for my back, I can't work.
> **Pharmacist:** I am sorry, back pain really affects the whole day. How long has it been like this?
> **Customer:** Since yesterday, after carrying boxes.
> **Pharmacist:** So it started after lifting, and it is stopping you from working. Is there any numbness in the legs, fever or trouble passing urine?
> **Customer:** No, only pain.
> **Pharmacist:** Good, thank you. I will suggest the safest option for you and explain how to use it. If the pain is not better in a few days or any of those signs appear, please see a doctor.

## 5. Listening in a busy branch

- **Acknowledge the next customer** with a nod or "I will be with you in a moment" so nobody feels invisible.
- **Do not interrupt** one customer to answer another unless it is an emergency. If you must, apologise and say how long you need.
- **Share the load.** Ask a colleague to take simple purchases while you counsel someone who needs time.
- **Reduce noise.** Lower the volume of the screen or radio, and move a long conversation to the end of the counter.
- **Use the phone wisely.** A customer standing in front of you has priority over a non-urgent call; ask the caller to hold briefly or call back.

## 6. Hearing the red flags

Active listening also protects safety. Words that call for a referral to a doctor or urgent care instead of a product include: chest pain, difficulty breathing, blood in stool or vomit, sudden severe headache, a high fever in a young infant, signs of allergic swelling, symptoms in pregnancy that worry the customer, or symptoms that keep returning. Follow the Lotus SOP and refer calmly, explaining why.

## 7. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Finishing the customer's sentence | Wait, then reflect |
| Looking at the screen while the customer talks | Pause and look up |
| Reassuring too early ("it is nothing") | Ask, then reassure only what is known |
| Giving advice before asking who the medicine is for | Always confirm patient, age, allergies first |
| Answering the phone mid-conversation without excuse | Apologise briefly and keep it short |

## Checklist: a good listening moment

- I stopped what I was doing and looked at the customer.
- I asked one open question.
- I let the answer finish.
- I reflected the main point.
- I asked the safety questions.
- I used one empathy statement.
- I summarised before I advised.

## Scenario

A branch is crowded in the evening. A mother says, quickly, "Give me the syrup for my son, he has a cough." Two people are waiting behind her. A weak approach would be to hand over a cough syrup. A better approach: "Of course. How old is he, and how long has he been coughing?" She answers that he is four and has coughed for a week with a bit of wheezing at night. The pharmacist reflects, "A week of cough, worse at night, with some wheezing, you must be tired." Because of the child's age, the length of the cough and the wheeze, the pharmacist does not suggest a syrup but explains calmly that the child should be seen by a doctor soon, and tells the next customer "thank you for waiting" while writing the advice on a small note for the mother. The listening took one minute and changed the outcome.

## Key takeaways

- Listening is how a pharmacist collects the information needed for safe counselling.
- Aim for active, empathic listening whenever symptoms, children or worry are involved.
- Open question first, then reflect, then ask focused safety questions, then summarise.
- Short sincere empathy statements work better than false reassurance.
- In a busy branch, acknowledge waiting customers and avoid interrupting a conversation.
- Listen for red flags and refer to a doctor calmly when they appear.
