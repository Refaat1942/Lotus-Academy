## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Use body language, facial expression and tone of voice to support, not contradict, your words
2. Handle telephone calls to the pharmacy with a clear opening, structure and closing
3. Write professional WhatsApp messages using approved templates and clear response times
4. Protect customer privacy on phone and messaging channels
5. Recognise requests that must not be handled by message and redirect them safely

---

## 1. The message beyond the words

Customers judge the pharmacy within seconds, often before you speak. Tone, face and posture can build trust or destroy it. When words and body language disagree, people usually believe the body language. A pharmacist who says "of course, I am happy to help" with crossed arms and a flat voice sounds uninterested.

## 2. Body language at the counter

| Element | Helpful | Unhelpful |
|---|---|---|
| Eye contact | Natural and brief, returning often | Staring, or avoiding eyes completely |
| Facial expression | Calm, a light smile when appropriate | Frowning, rolling eyes, looking bored |
| Posture | Upright, facing the customer, hands visible | Crossed arms, leaning on the shelf, turned away |
| Distance | Respectful, not too close | Standing too close or shouting across the counter |
| Hands | Open gestures, pointing to the product clearly | Fidgeting, tapping, playing with the phone |
| Appearance | Clean uniform, visible Lotus badge | Untidy uniform, no badge |

Be aware of cultural sensitivity. Some customers, for example some women or older men, may prefer a little more distance and less direct eye contact. Follow the customer's lead and stay respectful.

## 3. Tone and pace of voice

- **Volume:** loud enough to be heard over the branch noise, but not so loud that others hear private health details.
- **Pace:** slightly slower than normal speech when giving instructions.
- **Warmth:** a warm, steady tone. Avoid sarcasm and a rushed, irritated tone, even at the end of a long shift.
- **Pauses:** a short pause after each key point gives time to absorb.
- **Greeting:** a clear welcome such as "Welcome to Lotus, how can I help you?" sets the tone.

A useful check: if a recording of your voice were played back to the customer, would they feel respected?

## 4. Telephone communication

### Structure of a call

1. **Answer within a few rings** with a standard greeting: pharmacy name, branch, and your role.
2. **Ask how you can help** and listen without interrupting.
3. **Clarify** who the medicine is for and the key facts, using the same questions as at the counter.
4. **Answer or explain what you will do**, for example check stock, call back, or advise a visit.
5. **Repeat the key points** (what, when, where).
6. **Close politely** and wait for the customer to finish before hanging up.

### Model call

> **Pharmacist:** Good morning, Lotus Pharmacy, how can I help you?
> **Customer:** Do you have this medicine? I will send a photo.
> **Pharmacist:** Of course, please send it and I will check the stock now. May I put you on hold for one minute?
> **Pharmacist:** Thank you for waiting. Yes, it is available. Would you like us to keep it for you until this evening or arrange delivery?

### Phone rules

- Do not give a diagnosis or dose advice for a serious problem by phone without enough information. Ask the customer to come in or to see a doctor when needed.
- Ask permission before putting a caller on hold and do not leave them for more than a minute without returning.
- If the caller is angry, use the calming approach from Lesson 10.5.
- Never discuss one customer's details in a way that other customers in the branch can hear.

## 5. WhatsApp communication

WhatsApp is used by many Lotus customers for orders, availability questions and delivery. It is fast but informal, and informality causes mistakes. Use only the channel and the approved account that the Lotus SOP allows, never a personal number or personal account.

### Principles

- **Respond within the standard time** set by the branch. If you cannot answer yet, send a short acknowledgement: "We received your message and will reply shortly."
- **Be clear and short.** One idea per message, no slang, no voice notes with long medical explanations.
- **Use plain Arabic and avoid emojis** that look careless; a simple greeting is enough.
- **Confirm the details**: product name, strength, quantity, delivery address, payment method and price in EGP.
- **Do not send clinical advice for complex issues in writing.** Invite the customer to call or visit.
- **Check before sending**: right customer, right product, no typing mistakes in the dose.

### Message templates

**Greeting and acknowledgement:**
"Welcome to Lotus Pharmacy. Thank you for your message. We will check and reply shortly."

**Availability confirmation:**
"The product you asked about is available. The price is [amount] EGP. Would you like delivery or pick-up?"

**Not available:**
"Unfortunately this item is not available in our branch now. We can check another Lotus branch or suggest a suitable alternative after confirming with the prescriber or the pharmacist."

**Order confirmation:**
"Your order: [items and quantities], total [amount] EGP. Delivery to [area]. Expected time: [time]. Please confirm."

**Redirect to a call or visit:**
"For your safety we prefer to discuss this by phone or at the pharmacy. Please call us or visit when convenient."

### Privacy on messaging

- Do not send or forward a customer's prescription, photo or health details to anyone outside the Lotus process.
- Do not mention one customer's medicines in a group chat that includes people who do not need to know.
- Delete screenshots from personal phones and follow the SOP for storing messages.
- Verify you are speaking to the right person before sharing order details, especially for a sensitive item. If unsure, ask them to collect it in person.
- Never share bank or card details by message beyond what the approved payment process allows.

## 6. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Replying hours later with no acknowledgement | Send a quick holding message |
| Long voice notes of medical advice | Short text or a phone call |
| Answering the phone with a sigh or silence | Standard greeting, warm tone |
| Using a personal number | Use the approved Lotus channel only |
| Sending a prescription photo to a friend for advice | Use the proper consultation process |
| Wrong product in the order confirmation | Re-read before sending |

## Checklist: before pressing send

- Is this the right customer and the right product?
- Are dose, quantity and price correct?
- Is the language clear, polite and free of jargon?
- Does it contain private details that are not needed?
- Would I be comfortable if the customer's family read it?

## Scenario

On a Friday evening a branch receives a WhatsApp message with a photo of a prescription and the words "Is it available? Urgent." The technician sends the acknowledgement template within a minute, checks the stock, and replies with availability and price. The customer then asks, "Can I give this to my little boy together with the cough syrup I bought yesterday?" The pharmacist recognises that this question needs full information and should not be handled by short messages. The reply: "To answer safely we need a few details. Please call the branch or visit and the pharmacist will help you now." During the call, the pharmacist asks about the child's age and the exact products, and gives advice following the SOP.

## Key takeaways

- Body language and tone must support your words; when they conflict, customers believe the body language.
- Keep a calm, warm voice at a moderate pace and protect private details from nearby customers.
- Structure phone calls: greet, clarify, answer, repeat key points, close politely.
- On WhatsApp, use the approved account, templates and response times; confirm product, quantity and price in EGP.
- Do not give complex clinical advice by message; redirect to a call or a visit.
- Protect privacy: no forwarding prescriptions, no health details in group chats, verify the person first.
