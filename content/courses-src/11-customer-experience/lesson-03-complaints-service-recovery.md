## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain why a complaint is an opportunity and not an attack.
2. Apply the Listen-Apologise-Solve-Thank (LAST) framework in a real conversation.
3. Take ownership of a complaint and act within your empowerment limits.
4. Escalate correctly when a case is beyond your authority or involves product safety.
5. Document complaints so that the branch can learn from them.
6. Handle typical complaints: wrong item, product quality concern, long wait, price disagreement and delivery delay.

---

## 1. Why complaints matter

Most unhappy customers never complain. They simply stop coming. A customer who tells us about a problem is giving us a second chance. Handled well, a complaint can turn a disappointed customer into a loyal one. Handled badly, it spreads: the customer tells family and neighbours.

Remember: the customer is upset with a situation, not with you personally. Staying calm is a professional skill.

## 2. The LAST framework

| Step | What to do | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| **L**isten | Let the customer finish. Do not interrupt or defend. Nod, take notes, ask one or two clarifying questions. | "Please tell me what happened, I am listening." |
| **A**pologise | Apologise sincerely for the experience, without blaming a colleague or the customer. | "I am sorry this happened. I understand how frustrating it is." |
| **S**olve | Offer a clear solution or the next step, with a time. Ask if it works for the customer. | "I will replace it now. Would that be suitable for you?" |
| **T**hank | Thank the customer for raising it and confirm any follow-up. | "Thank you for telling us. It helps us improve." |

**Arabic phrases that help**

- "أنا آسف على اللي حصل، وهتابع الموضوع بنفسي."
- "حضرتك عندك حق إنك تتضايق."
- "هحل الموضوع دلوقتي، وده اللي هعمله."
- "شكرًا لأنك عرّفتنا، ده بيساعدنا نتحسن."

Avoid: "That is not my fault", "That is the policy", "You should have checked", "Calm down", and "It never happens".

## 3. Ownership and empowerment

**Ownership** means the person who receives the complaint owns it until it is resolved or properly handed over. Never say "that is not my department" and walk away. Say "I will take care of this" and either solve it or introduce the right colleague personally, explaining the case so the customer does not have to repeat the story.

**Empowerment limits** are set by the Lotus SOP and the pharmacy manager. As an example, a staff member may usually:

- apologise and explain,
- exchange a wrong item for the correct one,
- correct a clear counter or pricing error,
- offer a small courtesy such as free delivery on the next order, if the SOP allows.

Decisions about refunds above a set amount, exceptions to returns policy, and anything involving the safety or quality of a medicine go to the pharmacist in charge or the manager. Never promise something you are not authorised to give.

## 4. Escalation and safety

Escalate immediately when:

- the customer reports a possible **adverse reaction** or health harm. The pharmacist handles this and follows the current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority / Ministry of Health and the Lotus SOP for reporting;
- there is a concern about a product's **quality, packaging, expiry or storage**. Put the batch aside, do not sell it, and inform the pharmacist in charge;
- the customer is abusive or threatening. Protect yourself and your colleagues, stay calm, and call the manager;
- the customer asks for a decision you cannot make.

When escalating, say what will happen and when: "I will ask the pharmacist in charge to review this now. It will take about five minutes."

## 5. Documenting the complaint

Write a short record for every complaint, even if solved on the spot. Include the date and time, branch, a short description in the customer's words, the product or order involved, what was done, who handled it, and whether follow-up is needed. No judgment, no blame. This record lets the manager find patterns, such as the same supplier issue or a recurring delay at the same hour.

## 6. Typical complaints and model responses

**Wrong item given**

Customer: "You gave me a different strength."
Pharmacist: "I am sorry for the mistake, thank you for checking. Let me correct it right now." Check the prescription, give the correct item, check nothing was used, and report the error through the Lotus process so it does not repeat. If the customer already used any of it, involve the pharmacist and advise a doctor visit if there is any concern.

**Expired or damaged product concern**

Customer: "This box is damaged."
Pharmacist: "Thank you for telling me. I am sorry. I will replace it with a sound box immediately." Inspect the product, remove the batch from sale, record the batch number and inform the pharmacist in charge. Do not argue about whether it was damaged before or after purchase. If any product has been used and the customer has a health concern, advise contacting a doctor.

**Long wait**

Customer: "I have been waiting for twenty minutes."
Pharmacist: "I am sorry for the wait, thank you for your patience. I will serve you now." Then solve quickly and, if appropriate, mention what the branch is doing to reduce waiting.

**Price disagreement**

Customer: "It was cheaper last time."
Pharmacist: "Let me check this for you." Check the shelf and system. If the price is correct, explain calmly that prices can change and show the current price. If there was a labelling error, apologise and correct it. Never argue; offer options such as a different size or an equivalent product, only within the rules.

**Delivery delay**

Customer: "My order is late."
Pharmacist: "I am sorry. Let me check where it is now." Call the courier, give the customer a real new time, and offer the agreed courtesy if the SOP allows. Call the customer back when it is delivered.

## 7. Turning a complaint into loyalty

The service recovery paradox is real in many pharmacies: customers who see a problem solved quickly and kindly often become more loyal. Three habits help:

1. **Speed.** Act now, not tomorrow.
2. **Sincerity.** A real apology, not a script read without feeling.
3. **Follow-up.** A short call or message the next day: "I wanted to make sure everything is fine." This single step often creates the biggest effect.

## Common mistakes

- Defending the pharmacy before understanding the problem.
- Blaming a colleague, the supplier or the customer.
- Promising what you cannot deliver.
- Making the customer repeat the story to several people.
- Not recording the complaint.
- Treating a product quality concern as "just a complaint" instead of a safety matter.

## Scenario

A customer returns in the evening holding a skin cream and says loudly: "The seal was broken when I opened it. This is unacceptable."

**Model approach.** The pharmacist invites the customer to the side to talk calmly and listens without interrupting. Then: "I am sorry. You are right to raise this, and I will deal with it now." The pharmacist inspects the product, offers a replacement from a sealed stock, and takes the opened one for the pharmacist in charge to review. The batch details are recorded and the remaining units of the same batch are checked and set aside if the SOP requires. The customer is asked if they have had any skin reaction and, if so, is advised to stop use and see a doctor or dermatologist. The pharmacist thanks the customer and says the manager will call the next day to confirm everything is well. The customer leaves reassured because the issue was taken seriously.

## Key takeaways

- A complaint is a second chance to earn trust.
- Use LAST: Listen, Apologise, Solve, Thank.
- Own the complaint until it is resolved or properly handed over.
- Act within your empowerment limits and never promise what you cannot give.
- Escalate safety, quality and abusive-behaviour cases immediately.
- Record every complaint without blame so the branch can learn.
- Speed, sincerity and follow-up turn complaints into loyalty.
