## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Collect customer feedback in simple, respectful ways.
2. Explain and calculate a satisfaction score and a Net Promoter Score.
3. Read customer comments and group them into themes.
4. Close the loop by telling customers what was done.
5. Run a short team routine that turns feedback into improvement.
6. Describe how loyalty is built through consistent service.

---

## 1. Why measure?

"We think customers are happy" is not enough. A busy branch can feel successful while customers quietly leave. Feedback tells us what customers see, and measures tell us whether we are improving. The goal is **not** to judge individuals. The goal is to find what to fix and what to repeat.

## 2. Ways to collect feedback

| Method | How it works | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Face to face | A natural question at the end of a visit: "Was everything clear and easy today?" | Ask only when the customer is not rushed. |
| Short survey | One to three questions by message after an order or delivery | Short surveys get more replies. |
| Comment card or QR form as set by the branch | The customer writes freely | Make it simple and keep it visible. |
| Phone follow-up | A short call after a prescription or a complaint | Be brief and friendly. |
| Complaints and compliments log | Everything customers tell us | Record compliments too. |
| Staff observations | What the team notices: repeated questions, queues at certain hours | Share in team meetings. |

Rules for collecting feedback: always ask politely, accept that answering is optional, never pressure a customer for a high score, never ask for private health details in a survey, and keep answers confidential.

## 3. Simple satisfaction measures

**Customer satisfaction score (CSAT).** Ask: "How satisfied were you with your visit today?" using a scale of 1 to 5. Count the customers who answered 4 or 5 and divide by all who answered.

*Example:* 40 customers answered. 30 gave 4 or 5. Satisfaction score = 30 ÷ 40 = 75%.

**Net Promoter Score (NPS).** Ask: "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend this pharmacy to family or friends?"

- 9 or 10 are **promoters**.
- 7 or 8 are **passives**.
- 0 to 6 are **detractors**.

NPS = percentage of promoters minus percentage of detractors. The result is between minus 100 and plus 100.

*Example:* 50 answers. 30 promoters (60%), 12 passives (24%), 8 detractors (16%). NPS = 60 minus 16 = 44.

**Simple operational measures** also help: average waiting time at peak, number of complaints per week, percentage of orders delivered on time, and percentage of messages answered within the standard.

Important cautions:

- A few answers can mislead. Look at trends over weeks, not a single day.
- Compare a branch with its own past, not unfairly with other branches in different areas.
- Numbers show what happened. Comments explain why.
- Do not invent targets. Targets are set by the Lotus management.

## 4. Reading comments

Comments are the most valuable part. To read them well:

1. **Collect** all comments for the period.
2. **Group them into themes:** waiting time, staff manner, availability, price, delivery, cleanliness, advice.
3. **Count** how many comments fall in each theme.
4. **Separate** one-off events from patterns.
5. **Look for safety signals,** such as a product quality concern or a possible reaction, and pass them to the pharmacist in charge immediately.
6. **Pick one or two** themes to improve. Too many actions means none are done.

| Comment (summarised) | Theme |
|---|---|
| "I waited a long time in the evening." | Waiting time |
| "The pharmacist explained everything clearly." | Advice (positive) |
| "The product I wanted was not there." | Availability |
| "The courier did not call before arriving." | Delivery |

Praise is also data. When a customer praises a service, share it with the team and repeat what worked.

## 5. Closing the loop

Closing the loop means telling the customer what we did with their feedback. It proves that we listen.

1. **Thank** the customer for the feedback.
2. **Act** on what is within the branch's control.
3. **Tell** the customer: "You told us about long waits in the evening. We now have an extra team member from six to eight o'clock."
4. **Tell the team** too, so they know their effort mattered.

For an unhappy customer who gave a low score, call or message within a short time (for example within a day), listen, apologise, and offer to put things right. This is service recovery from Lesson 11.3 applied to feedback.

Script: "Thank you for your feedback. I am sorry your experience was not what it should be. I would like to understand more so that we can fix it. May I ask a few questions?"

## 6. Loyalty

Loyalty is not only a card or a discount. It is the result of repeated good experiences: being recognised, being advised honestly, finding products, getting orders on time, and being treated fairly when something goes wrong.

Ways to support loyalty within Lotus rules:

- remember regular customers' preferences with their agreement, and treat the information as confidential;
- remind customers about refills of long-term medicines through the approved process, never by pressure to buy;
- explain any loyalty programme clearly and honestly, without exaggerated promises;
- thank returning customers by recognising their time;
- keep promises on delivery and availability.

A loyal customer recommends the pharmacy to family and neighbours. In Egypt, a recommendation from someone you trust is one of the strongest forms of advertising.

## 7. A team routine for improvement

Hold a **ten-minute weekly huddle** with this agenda:

1. Share one compliment received this week.
2. Review the top two themes in feedback and complaints.
3. Choose **one** improvement for next week, with an owner and a day to check it.
4. Check last week's action: did it help?
5. Record the decision and thank the team.

**Checklist for the manager**

- Is feedback collected every week?
- Are comments read and grouped, not only counted?
- Is each unhappy customer contacted?
- Are safety concerns passed to the pharmacist in charge?
- Is every improvement given an owner and a date?
- Has the customer been told what changed?

## Common mistakes

- Collecting feedback and never acting on it.
- Pressuring customers to give high scores.
- Using scores to blame an individual.
- Judging by one day or a few answers.
- Ignoring comments and watching only the number.
- Choosing too many improvements at once.
- Treating loyalty as discounts only.

## Scenario

For two weeks, the evening surveys at a branch show lower satisfaction. Comments mention "waiting" nine times, "price" twice and "staff manner" once.

**Model approach.** The manager groups the comments and sees that waiting is the clear theme. At the weekly huddle, the team looks at when queues form and finds that two team members take breaks during the busiest hour. They choose one action: move breaks and place a team member at the counter to handle quick purchases from six to eight. They set a check for the following week. The manager also calls two customers who left low scores, listens, apologises and thanks them. A short note on the counter tells customers: "You told us about waiting. We have added support in the evening." The next survey is compared with the previous one to see if it helped.

## Key takeaways

- Feedback shows what customers see; measures show whether we improve.
- Collect feedback politely and in simple ways, and never pressure customers.
- Satisfaction score is the share of 4 or 5 answers. NPS is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors.
- Group comments into themes and look for patterns, not single events.
- Pass any safety signal to the pharmacist in charge at once.
- Close the loop by telling customers and the team what changed.
- A weekly ten-minute routine with one action, an owner and a date drives real improvement.
