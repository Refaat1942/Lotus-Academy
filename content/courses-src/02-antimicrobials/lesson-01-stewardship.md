## 1. Antimicrobial stewardship framework

### 1.1 Core principles

Antimicrobial stewardship means using the right antimicrobial, only when it is needed, at the right dose, for the right duration. Every antibiotic dispensed in a Lotus branch either protects or weakens the effectiveness of antibiotics for the whole community.

The four goals of stewardship are:

- **Improve patient outcomes** — the patient gets better, faster and more safely
- **Reduce antimicrobial resistance (AMR)** — fewer unnecessary courses mean fewer resistant bacteria
- **Minimise adverse effects** — for example C. difficile diarrhoea and nephrotoxicity
- **Control healthcare costs** — for the patient, the family and the health system

### 1.2 WHO AWaRe classification

| Category | Meaning | Egyptian examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Access** | First or second choice for common infections — should be widely available | Amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, cephalexin, trimethoprim, clindamycin |
| **Watch** | Higher resistance potential — use should be restricted and justified | Ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, clarithromycin, ceftriaxone |
| **Reserve** | Last-line — for confirmed or suspected multidrug-resistant infections, specialist use | Meropenem, colistin, linezolid |

**Evidence:** WHO Global Action Plan on AMR (2015); the Egyptian National AMR Action Plan is aligned with the WHO plan.

### 1.3 Egyptian resistance data (summary)

The figures below come from published Egyptian surveillance and hospital studies. They vary by study, city and setting, so use them to understand the direction of the problem, not as exact local rates.

| Pathogen | Resistance concern (Egypt surveillance) |
|---|---|
| E. coli (UTI) | ESBL production reported in about 30–50% of hospital isolates; high fluoroquinolone resistance |
| Klebsiella | Carbapenem resistance emerging in ICU settings |
| S. aureus | MRSA reported in 20–40% of isolates in some hospital surveys |
| S. pneumoniae | Penicillin and macrolide resistance variable |
| M. tuberculosis | MDR-TB in about 3% of new cases (WHO Egypt estimate) |

### 1.4 EDA regulatory framework

| Rule | Pharmacy implication |
|---|---|
| Antimicrobials are prescription-only | Do not dispense without a valid prescription |
| Retention of prescriptions and records | Keep prescription copies and records for the period required by current EDA / Ministry of Health rules and the Lotus SOP |
| Controlled antimicrobials (TB drugs, some IV agents) | Supplied through hospital, specialist or national programme channels |
| OTC misuse concern | Nifuroxazide and some antidiarrhoeals are sold OTC and often used instead of oral rehydration — a stewardship issue |

**Counselling script (Arabic):**

> "المضاد الحيوي بيعالج البكتيريا بس، مش الفيروسات زي البرد والإنفلونزا. لازم تكمل الكورس كله حتى لو حسيت بتحسن. متشاركش المضاد مع حد تاني ومتستخدمش مضاد قديم من عندك. ده بيخلي البكتيريا تقاوم الدوا."

In English: antibiotics treat bacteria only, not viruses such as colds and flu. Finish the whole course even if you feel better. Do not share antibiotics and do not use old leftover antibiotics — this helps bacteria become resistant.

## 2. Pharmacist stewardship interventions

The pharmacist is the last professional check before the antibiotic reaches the patient. Each prescription is an opportunity for one of these interventions:

| Intervention | Example |
|---|---|
| Validate indication | Question a fluoroquinolone for uncomplicated UTI when nitrofurantoin or cephalexin may be appropriate — contact the prescriber, do not change the prescription yourself |
| Duration check | Amoxicillin 5–7 days for otitis media; not 14 days routinely |
| Drug interaction screening | Clarithromycin + statin → myopathy; metronidazole + warfarin → raised INR |
| Patient education | Arabic counselling on adherence, timing and resistance |
| Referral | Persistent fever for more than 48 hours on antibiotics → physician |

Common mistakes at the counter:

- Selling "a short course" of antibiotic to a regular customer to avoid an argument
- Accepting "the doctor told me on the phone" without a prescription
- Not asking about other medicines, pregnancy or allergies
- Saying "take it until you feel better" instead of explaining the full course

## 3. Pharmacy practice scenario

### Scenario 2.1 — Viral URI antibiotic request

> A mother requests amoxicillin for her 4-year-old with a runny nose and cough for 2 days and no fever. She says the neighbour's child got the same antibiotic.

**Model response:**

1. Explain that these symptoms are most likely viral and antibiotics will not help
2. Recommend supportive care: saline nasal drops, paracetamol at the correct weight-based dose if fever develops, plenty of fluids and rest
3. Give clear red flags for referral to a doctor: fever above 39°C, difficulty breathing or fast breathing, poor feeding or drowsiness, ear pain, or symptoms lasting more than 10 days
4. Do NOT dispense an antibiotic without a prescription, regardless of pressure
5. Document the counselling provided according to the Lotus SOP

Pharmacist: "I understand you want him to get better quickly. This is a cold, and antibiotics do not work on cold viruses — they could only give him diarrhoea. These drops and fluids will help him feel better. If he gets a high fever, breathes fast or is not better in a few days, please take him to the doctor."

## Key takeaways

- AWaRe: Access antibiotics are first line; Watch and Reserve antibiotics need justification
- Egyptian E. coli UTI: high fluoroquinolone resistance — avoid empiric ciprofloxacin when possible
- Pharmacists must enforce prescription-only rules — no antibiotic dispensing without a prescription
- OTC misuse of nifuroxazide (for example instead of ORS) is a stewardship concern in Egypt
- Counselling to complete the prescribed course prevents relapse and partial treatment
- Under the Egyptian National AMR Plan, the pharmacy is a frontline of stewardship
