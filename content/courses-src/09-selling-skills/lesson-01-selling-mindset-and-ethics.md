## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain what consultative selling means in a pharmacy and how it differs from pushing products
2. Apply the patient-first principle when a customer's need and the pharmacy's target point in different directions
3. Describe the boundaries of OTC advice and name situations where you must not sell
4. Distinguish the professional role of the pharmacist from that of a pure salesperson
5. Use simple self-check questions before every recommendation

---

## 1. Why pharmacy selling is different

A customer in a shop buys what they want. A customer in a pharmacy often buys what they fear, a pain, a child's fever, a skin problem, a worry about a parent's blood pressure. They trust us because they cannot judge the product themselves. That trust is the pharmacy's real asset, and every sale either adds to it or spends it.

**Consultative selling** means we start from the customer's situation, ask, listen, and then recommend what fits. **Pushing** means we start from the product we want to move and look for a customer to attach it to. The customer feels the difference within seconds, especially in Egypt where families stay with "their" pharmacy for years and talk to neighbours about it.

| Pushing | Consultative |
|---|---|
| Starts with the product | Starts with the person |
| Talks first | Asks first |
| Same offer for everyone | Offer fits this customer |
| One sale today | Relationship over years |
| Customer feels managed | Customer feels cared for |

## 2. The patient-first principle

When the need of the patient and a sales target or a high-margin product conflict, the patient wins. This is not only ethics; it is also business sense. A customer who discovers they were over-sold does not come back, and tells others.

Practical rules:

- Recommend the **simplest effective option** first, not the most expensive.
- Never suggest a product the customer does not need.
- Be honest when a product will not solve the problem.
- Never create fear to make a sale.
- Never promise a cure, a guaranteed result or a speed that the product cannot deliver.

## 3. Boundaries of OTC advice

Pharmacists counsel; they do not diagnose. Follow the current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority and the Ministry of Health and the Lotus SOP on what may be dispensed without a prescription. Beyond that, apply professional limits:

- Do not dispense a prescription-only medicine without a valid prescription, however much the customer insists or "knows" the drug.
- Do not recommend a medicine for a condition you have not understood properly.
- Take extra care with children, pregnancy and breastfeeding, the elderly, and people on several medicines or with chronic disease.
- Do not suggest a change to a doctor's prescribed treatment. Refer the customer back to the doctor.

## 4. When NOT to sell

There are moments when the right action is to sell nothing, or to sell less:

1. **Red-flag symptoms** that need a doctor, for example severe or worsening pain, a high fever that persists, difficulty breathing, blood in stool or vomit, a child who is very drowsy, sudden severe headache. Refer calmly and clearly.
2. **Repeated self-treatment** where the problem returns again and again.
3. **Possible misuse** such as repeated requests for sedatives or strong painkillers.
4. **No real need**, such as a customer asking for an antibiotic "just in case" for a cold. Explain gently that antibiotics are for a doctor to decide.
5. **A cheaper or simpler solution exists**, such as a customer asking for an expensive product when a basic one is enough.

*Model phrase (referral):*

> Pharmacist: "From what you describe, I would like a doctor to examine you before taking anything. It is safer, and I do not want you to spend money on something that may not help. If you like, I can explain what to tell the doctor."

Referring a customer is not losing a sale. It is how a customer decides we are the pharmacy to trust.

## 5. Pharmacist versus salesperson

| | Salesperson only | Professional pharmacist |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | "What can I sell?" | "What does this person need?" |
| Success measure | Today's receipt | Customer's outcome and return visit |
| When unsure | Sell anyway | Check, ask, refer |
| Product knowledge | Prices and offers | Indications, cautions, interactions, alternatives |
| Attitude to "no" | A failure | A legitimate result |

Sales skills do not conflict with professionalism. They are the communication tools that let good advice be heard and accepted. A pharmacist who gives perfect advice in a cold voice may lose the customer to a friendlier but less careful source.

## 6. The five-second self-check

Before every recommendation, ask yourself:

1. Do I understand what this customer actually needs?
2. Is this the simplest option that works?
3. Is it safe for this person (age, pregnancy, other medicines, allergies)?
4. Would I be comfortable explaining my reason in front of the customer's family?
5. If it were my own relative, would I recommend the same thing?

If any answer is "no", stop and ask more questions or refer.

## 7. Common mistakes

- Reacting to the target of the month instead of the customer.
- Recommending the product with the best margin without asking about the problem.
- Using fear: "If you do not take this, it will get worse."
- Agreeing to anything to avoid an argument at a crowded counter.
- Treating every question as a chance to sell something, including when the customer only wants information.
- Speaking about doctors or other pharmacies in a critical way.

## Scenario

A mother comes in during the evening rush asking for "the strongest syrup" for her child's cough, which started last night. The branch is crowded and a bundle of cough products is on promotion.

**Model approach.** The pharmacist calmly sets the promotion aside and asks: the child's age, how long the cough has lasted, any fever, breathing difficulty, wheeze, medicines already given, and whether the child is eating and drinking. The child is very young and the mother mentions fast breathing. This is a red flag. The pharmacist says clearly and kindly that the child should be seen by a doctor today, explains what to tell the doctor, and offers only simple supportive advice that is appropriate. No product is sold. The mother thanks the pharmacist and returns next week for her own items and family purchases. The sale lost that evening was small; the trust gained was large.

## Key takeaways

- Consultative selling starts with the person; pushing starts with the product.
- Patient first: the simplest effective and safe option comes before margin or targets.
- Pharmacists counsel and refer; they never diagnose or bypass prescription rules.
- Red flags, repeated self-treatment, possible misuse and no real need are moments not to sell.
- Referral and "you do not need this" build long-term trust and loyalty.
- Use the five-second self-check before every recommendation.
