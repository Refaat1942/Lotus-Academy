## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Explain why an objection is usually a request for information or reassurance, not a rejection
2. Apply a four-step method (listen, acknowledge, respond, check) to any objection
3. Handle price objections in EGP, including inflation, with honest options
4. Respond respectfully to "someone recommended another product", "I will think about it" and trust objections
5. Recognise when to stop persuading and let the customer decide or leave

---

## 1. What an objection really is

An objection is the customer telling you something is not yet clear or comfortable. Typical hidden meanings:

| What the customer says | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| "It is expensive." | "I am not sure it is worth it" or "My budget is limited." |
| "I will think about it." | "I do not understand enough", "I do not trust yet", or "I cannot afford it now." |
| "Someone told me to use another one." | "I trust that person; can you give me a reason to trust you?" |
| "Is this really necessary?" | "I fear being over-sold." |

Treat objections as part of the conversation, never as an attack. Arguing wins the point and loses the customer.

## 2. The four-step method: LARC

1. **Listen.** Let the customer finish. Do not interrupt or defend.
2. **Acknowledge.** Show you heard: "I understand, prices have gone up for everything."
3. **Respond.** Give honest information, an option, or a reason.
4. **Check.** Ask: "Does that make sense?" or "Which would suit you better?"

If the objection is unclear, ask first: "What worries you most about this one?"

## 3. Price objections in EGP

Many customers feel the effect of inflation in every receipt and compare with what they paid before. Do not be defensive and do not pretend prices have not changed. Instead:

- Acknowledge honestly.
- **Clarify the need.** Is the product essential (a prescribed medicine) or optional (a supplement or cosmetic)?
- **Offer choices**: a registered alternative with the same active ingredient where appropriate and allowed, a different pack size, or prioritising the essential item.
- **Compare value fairly**: cost per day or per dose can be clearer than the pack price. Use real numbers only from the shelf and never invent prices.
- Respect the customer's decision to buy less.

*Model dialogue 1 (price):*

> Customer: "This has become so expensive."
> Pharmacist: "True, I know prices have risen on everything, and I understand. Let me see what suits your budget. For this problem there is also another registered product with the same active ingredient and strength at a lower price. Would you like me to explain the difference?"
> Customer: "Yes."
> Pharmacist: "The main difference is the brand name and the packaging. Both are suitable for you. Which one do you prefer?"

*Model dialogue 2 (limited budget, several items):*

> Customer: "I cannot take all of this today."
> Pharmacist: "No problem. The first two are the important ones for the next few days. The vitamin can wait until next month if you wish."

Never push the customer to a credit-like arrangement or to buy a pack they cannot afford. Follow the Lotus SOP on any payment options.

## 4. "I will take the cheaper one"

This is a legitimate choice. Your task is to make sure it is a safe and informed choice.

1. Check that the cheaper product is **suitable for the same use, strength and person**.
2. Explain honestly any difference that matters: pack size, form, how often it is taken.
3. If there is no difference that matters, say so and sell it gladly.
4. If the cheaper one is not suitable, explain why in one sentence, without drama.

> Pharmacist: "That is fine. This one has the same active ingredient and strength, so it will do the same job for you. The only difference is that you take it three times daily rather than twice, so please set reminders. Shall I write the times on the box?"

## 5. "Someone recommended another product"

The "someone" may be a relative, a friend, a doctor, social media or an advertisement. Do not criticise them. Do not compete. Ask and compare against the customer's need.

> Customer: "My neighbour told me to use another one for this."
> Pharmacist: "Thank you for telling me. What did she say it helps with? ... Let me check whether it suits your case, since you have told me about your other medicine. If it is suitable and safe, there is no problem using it. If there is something to be careful about, I will explain."

Safety check first: look at the active ingredients, interactions, age and conditions. If the other product is suitable, support the customer's choice. If it is not suitable, explain why plainly. If it was a doctor's advice, follow the doctor.

## 6. "I will think about it"

Do not give in to pressure and do not push. Find out what remains unclear.

> Customer: "I will think about it."
> Pharmacist: "Of course, take your time. Is there something I can clarify to help you decide, such as the price, how it is used, or whether it suits you?"

If the customer still wants to leave:

- Summarise the key point in one line and when to return or see a doctor if symptoms continue.
- Offer to write the product name and the instructions, or to send it by message if the customer agrees.
- Never create false urgency such as "the price will go up tomorrow" unless it is true and relevant, and never use fear.

## 7. Trust objections

Examples: "Is this original?" "Why should I trust this offer?" "I had a bad experience with something like this."

- **Answer directly and transparently.** Show the box, batch and expiry date, and explain that the pharmacy buys from licensed sources according to Lotus procedures.
- **Do not take it personally.** Counterfeit and storage concerns are real worries for customers.
- **Acknowledge past experiences** without arguing.
- **Offer evidence you can support**, such as the leaflet or the registered information, not personal promises.
- If you cannot resolve the concern, tell the customer you will check and report back, or involve the pharmacist in charge.

## 8. When to stop persuading

- The customer has understood, has the facts and said no: accept it.
- The product is not essential and the budget is tight: accept it.
- The customer is distressed: help first, sell nothing.
- You feel your goal is the sale, not the customer's benefit: stop and re-read Lesson 9.1.

Customers return to pharmacists who let them decide.

## 9. Common mistakes

- Arguing or proving the customer wrong.
- Saying "this is the cheapest" without checking.
- Criticising a doctor, a neighbour or another pharmacy.
- Dropping the price without a real reason, which teaches customers to bargain on medicines.
- Creating false urgency or fear.
- Giving up too early on a safety issue because the customer objects.

## Scenario

A customer in his forties comes for a dermocosmetic moisturiser and a sunscreen that the pharmacist recommended for his dry, sensitive skin. At the till he says, "This is too expensive. And my cousin told me to use a different cream. I will think about it."

**Model approach.** The pharmacist stays calm and says, "I understand, prices have gone up everywhere. May I ask which cream your cousin mentioned, and what it is for?" After checking, the cream turns out to be suitable but not intended for sun protection. The pharmacist explains that the sunscreen is the more important item for his skin, offers a lower-priced moisturiser, and says the first one can wait. The customer buys the sunscreen and the simpler moisturiser, takes the instructions written on the box, and asks to come back with questions. No pressure, no fear, and a clear next step. A week later he returns for the other item.

## Key takeaways

- An objection is a request for clarity, reassurance or options, not a rejection.
- Use LARC: Listen, Acknowledge, Respond, Check.
- For price, acknowledge inflation honestly, separate essential from optional, and offer real options.
- "The cheaper one" is a valid choice if it is safe and suitable; make it an informed one.
- When someone else recommended a product, check it against the customer's need and support it if it is suitable.
- Answer "I will think about it" with an offer to clarify, never with pressure or false urgency.
- Accept a final "no" gracefully.
