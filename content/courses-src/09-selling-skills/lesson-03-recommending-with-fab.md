## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Build a recommendation using Features, Advantages and Benefits (FAB) that is tied to what the customer said
2. Present one main recommendation and, when appropriate, one alternative instead of overwhelming the customer
3. Explain generics versus brands honestly and without criticising either
4. Use evidence and product knowledge accurately without exaggeration
5. Give clear usage instructions and safety cautions with every recommendation

---

## 1. Recommend, do not list

After discovery, the customer expects a clear answer. Reading out five products leaves them confused, and confusion usually ends in "I will think about it" or "give me the cheapest". The best pattern is:

1. **Link** to what they told you: "You said the cough is dry and keeps you awake at night."
2. **Recommend** one main option, and at most one alternative at a different price point when it is relevant.
3. **Explain** with FAB.
4. **Instruct** how to use it and when to see a doctor.
5. **Check** with a question: "Does that sound suitable to you?"

## 2. Features, Advantages, Benefits

| Level | Question it answers | Example (general, a sugar-free throat product) |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | What is it? | It is sugar-free |
| **Advantage** | What does it do better or differently? | It does not add sugar to the diet |
| **Benefit** | What does it mean for *this* customer? | "You said you are watching your sugar, so you can use it without worrying about that." |

The customer buys the benefit. Features alone sound like a catalogue; benefits connect to the story the customer told you. That is why discovery must come first: without it you cannot choose the right benefit.

*Weak:* "This one is 100 mg, film coated, with a new formula."
*Strong:* "This one is film coated, so it is easier to swallow, and since you told me you find tablets hard to swallow, that should be more comfortable for you."

### Rules for using FAB honestly

- Choose **one to three** benefits that match the customer's words. Do not recite everything.
- Never turn a feature into a medical promise. "Gentle formula" must not become "it will not cause any problem."
- If the product has limits, say so: "It helps with the discomfort, but if it continues beyond a few days, please see a doctor."
- Mention important cautions in the same breath as the benefits.

## 3. Generics and brands, explained honestly

Customers in Egypt often ask: "Is the cheaper one the same?" or "Is the imported one better?" Answer in a way that is true, balanced and respectful of both options.

What you can say in general terms:

- A generic contains the **same active ingredient** as the original brand, in a corresponding strength and form, and is registered according to the requirements of the Egyptian Drug Authority.
- Differences can exist in the inactive ingredients, shape, taste, packaging, manufacturer and price. For most people these differences do not matter; for some (an allergy to an inactive ingredient, difficulty swallowing, a doctor's specific instruction) they may.
- Prices differ for many reasons, including research costs, brand, packaging and manufacturing; a lower price does not automatically mean lower quality, and a higher price does not automatically mean better.
- When a doctor wrote a specific brand, do not tell the customer to change it on your own. Follow the Lotus SOP and the rules on substitution, and contact the prescriber when needed.

*Model dialogue:*

> Customer: "Is this cheaper one really the same as the original?"
> Pharmacist: "It has the same active ingredient in the same strength, and it is registered here. The main differences are the brand name, the packaging and the price. For your case, either one is suitable. If you have trouble with the original, or the doctor wrote a particular brand, I will tell you honestly."

Do not say "the original is the only one that works", and do not say "they are exactly identical in everything." Both are untrue shortcuts.

Where the customer has had a bad experience with a product, listen and record it; do not argue. Experiences are real and may be useful information.

## 4. Using evidence and product knowledge

Customers respect a pharmacist who knows the product, and they notice when you read the box. Build your knowledge in these areas for the products you recommend often:

- What it is for, and what it is not for
- How to use it (dose, timing, with or without food, duration)
- Who should be careful: age, pregnancy, breastfeeding, chronic disease, other medicines
- Common side effects and when to stop and seek advice
- How quickly to expect improvement, in realistic terms
- What the leaflet and the supplier information actually claim

Rules for claims:

- Use only what the approved leaflet, the registered claims and reliable sources support.
- Do not quote studies or percentages you cannot verify. If you do not know, say: "Let me check that for you and tell you accurately."
- Distinguish *"this is commonly used for..."* from *"this will cure..."*.
- Supplier representatives are a useful source of information, but their messages are marketing. Check against the leaflet.
- Never compare by running down another product. Say what your recommendation does well.

## 5. Clear instructions close the recommendation

A recommendation is incomplete until the customer knows how to use it. Use a short, repeatable pattern:

1. **How much and how often**, written on the box if needed.
2. **For how long**, and when to stop.
3. **What to avoid** (for example other products with the same ingredient).
4. **What to expect** and by when.
5. **When to return or see a doctor.**

Use teach-back: "To make sure I explained clearly, how will you take it at home?" This is especially valuable for older customers and anyone buying for a child.

## 6. Common mistakes

- Reading out the leaflet or the price list instead of linking to the customer's words.
- Presenting too many products.
- Using technical jargon the customer does not understand.
- Overpromising: "This is the best", "This works for everyone."
- Criticising the doctor's brand choice or another pharmacy's product.
- Forgetting to give usage and warning information.
- Saying "the original is always better" or "generics are all the same" without nuance.

## Scenario

A customer in his sixties, who takes a regular tablet for blood pressure, asks for something for heartburn that started this week. He asks, "Which is better, the imported one at a higher price or the local one?"

**Model approach.** After discovery (no red flags such as chest pain, difficulty swallowing, black stools or weight loss, and the medicines he takes were checked), the pharmacist recommends one suitable option and explains with FAB: it is taken after meals, so it fits his routine; it is intended for short-term relief of occasional heartburn; he should avoid heavy late meals. When he asks about price, the pharmacist explains that the local product has the same active ingredient, strength and form, and that the main difference is brand and cost, so either is suitable for him. The pharmacist gives instructions, says to see a doctor if the heartburn continues beyond the period stated on the leaflet or returns often, and asks him to repeat how he will take it. The customer chooses the local product and leaves confident, having been told the truth about both.

## Key takeaways

- Recommend one main option (and at most one alternative), linked to the customer's own words.
- FAB: the feature is what it is, the advantage is what it does, the benefit is what it means for this customer.
- Be honest about generics and brands: same active ingredient does not mean identical in everything, and a higher price is not automatically better.
- Never exaggerate; use only supported claims and say "let me check" when unsure.
- Always give usage, cautions and a referral trigger with the recommendation.
- Use teach-back to confirm that the customer understood.

- a) To list the technical specifications of the product
- b) To mention the price of the product
- c) To describe how the product is manufactured
- d) To show what the feature means for this particular customer's need ✓

**Q2.** A customer has told you she cannot swallow large tablets. Which is the best FAB statement?
- a) "This is the newest product on the shelf."

- c) "This one is on promotion this week."
- d) "This is what most customers buy."

**Q3.** How many main options should you generally present after discovery?
- a) One main recommendation and at most one alternative when relevant ✓
- b) As many as possible so the customer chooses
- c) Five, in order of price
- d) None, until the customer asks

**Q4.** A customer asks whether a cheaper generic is the same as the original brand. What is the best honest answer?
- a) "No, only the original works."
- b) "They are identical in every respect."
- c) "It has the same active ingredient and strength and is registered; differences are mainly brand, packaging, inactive ingredients and price, and I will tell you if any matters for you." ✓
- d) "Ask the supplier."

**Q5.** A doctor prescribed a specific brand. The customer asks you to replace it with a cheaper one. What should you do?
- a) Replace it without informing anyone
- b) Tell the customer the doctor was wrong
- c) Refuse to discuss it

**Q6.** You are unsure about a claim a supplier representative made. What should you do?
- a) Repeat it to the customer confidently anyway

**Q7.** Which statement is an unacceptable exaggeration?
- a) "This is commonly used for temporary relief of this problem."
- b) "Please follow the leaflet and see a doctor if it continues."
- c) "This works for everybody and will cure it completely." ✓
- d) "Improvement may take a few days."

**Q8.** What is the purpose of teach-back?
- a) To test whether the customer knows the price
- b) To end the conversation faster
- c) To check that the customer understood how to use the product safely ✓

**Q9.** Which should be included with every recommendation?
- a) A comparison criticising other pharmacies
- b) Usage instructions, key cautions and when to see a doctor ✓
- c) The profit margin
- d) A guarantee of results

**Q10.** A customer says a generic once did not suit them. What is the best response?
- a) Listen, record the experience and take it into account when recommending ✓

- c) Insist that all generics are the same
