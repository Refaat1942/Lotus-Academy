## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Greet customers in a way that is warm, quick and appropriate for a busy Egyptian branch
2. Use open and closed questions in the right order to discover the real need
3. Distinguish symptom-led requests from product-led requests and respond to each correctly
4. Recognise red flags during discovery and refer the customer to a doctor
5. Practise active listening and confirm understanding before recommending

---

## 1. The first ten seconds

Customers decide quickly whether this is a pharmacy where they will be listened to. A good greeting is short, warm and makes the customer feel seen, even when the branch is crowded.

Checklist for the greeting:

- Eye contact and a smile as soon as the customer approaches, even if you are finishing another task.
- A simple Arabic greeting and an open invitation: "Welcome, how can I help you?" / "أهلًا بحضرتك، أقدر أساعد حضرتك بإيه؟"
- Keep the counter tidy, phone away from your hand, no side conversations with colleagues.
- If you are busy: acknowledge the new customer ("Just one minute and I am with you") so nobody feels ignored.
- Use respectful forms of address suitable for the customer's age, without using names you do not know.

*Common mistake:* starting with "Yes?" without looking up, or answering a phone call in the middle of counselling.

## 2. Two kinds of requests

| | Product-led request | Symptom-led request |
|---|---|---|
| Example | "Give me a box of X." | "My stomach hurts since yesterday." |
| What the customer has decided | The product | Only the problem |
| Risk | Product may be wrong, unsafe or unnecessary | Customer expects you to fix it quickly |
| Your first move | Confirm what it is for and for whom | Explore the symptom |

Even for a product-led request, ask a short confirming question: "Is it for you or someone else? What is it for?" It takes five seconds and prevents mistakes, especially with look-alike names, children and people on other medicines. Do not turn it into an interrogation for a simple repeat purchase of a well-known item such as plasters or a regular chronic medicine. Use judgement.

## 3. Open and closed questions

**Open questions** invite the customer to tell their story: "Tell me what is bothering you." "What have you tried so far?" They give information and build trust.

**Closed questions** need a yes/no or a short answer: "Any fever?" "Is the patient pregnant?" They confirm details and check safety.

The best order is **open first, then closed**:

1. Open: "What seems to be the problem?"
2. Open: "How long has it been like this?"
3. Closed: "Any fever, vomiting or rash?"
4. Closed: "Any allergies, or other medicines taken now?"
5. Open: "What have you tried already, and did it help?"

### The basic discovery frame

Use a simple memory aid, for example **WHAM**:

- **W**ho is it for? (age, pregnancy, breastfeeding)
- **H**ow long, and how severe?
- **A**ction taken already? (medicines tried)
- **M**edicines and conditions now? (chronic disease, allergies)

You do not need to say the letters aloud. They guide the conversation.

*Model dialogue (symptom-led):*

> Customer: "I have a bad sore throat."
> Pharmacist: "Sorry to hear that. How long has it been like this?"
> Customer: "Since two days."
> Pharmacist: "Any fever or difficulty swallowing food or water? Have you taken anything for it?"
> Customer: "A little fever yesterday. I took only a hot drink."
> Pharmacist: "Any allergies, or any medicines you take regularly?"

## 4. Listening before recommending

Most weak recommendations come from listening too little. Techniques:

- **Do not interrupt.** Let the customer finish the first description before asking.
- **Reflect back:** "So you have had a dry cough for three days, worse at night, without fever. Is that right?"
- **Notice what is not said:** hesitation about price, or embarrassment about intimate problems. Offer privacy gently: "If you prefer, we can talk at the side."
- **Listen to the person asking.** The customer may be describing a parent or child; check who the patient is.
- **Take brief notes** when the story is long, and write a clear note for phone and WhatsApp requests.

Customers often start with a small request and keep the real worry for later. "While you are here, I also wanted to ask about..." is a signal that trust has been built.

## 5. Red flags: when discovery ends in referral

During discovery, always listen for signals that need a doctor. Examples (general, not a full list): difficulty breathing, chest pain, severe or sudden headache, persistent high fever, blood in stool, urine or vomit, signs of dehydration in a child or elderly person, severe abdominal pain, symptoms in a very young infant, a wound that is spreading or very painful, symptoms that last longer than expected or keep returning, symptoms during pregnancy that worry the customer. Follow the current Lotus SOP and professional guidance.

How to refer:

1. Stay calm; do not alarm the customer.
2. State clearly: "I recommend that you see a doctor today."
3. Explain why in simple words.
4. Help: what to tell the doctor, what to bring (list of medicines).
5. If urgent, advise emergency care directly.

*Model phrase:* "I want to be careful with you. These symptoms should be examined by a doctor, and I would not like to give you something that hides the problem."

## 6. Summarise and confirm

Before recommending, summarise in one sentence and confirm: "So it is for your father, he is sixty-five, he takes a blood pressure tablet, and he has had heartburn for a week. Did I understand correctly?" This reassures the customer and catches mistakes. Then move to recommendation (Lesson 9.3).

## 7. Common mistakes

- Recommending after only one question.
- Asking closed questions only, so the customer feels interrogated.
- Not asking about other medicines and allergies.
- Assuming the customer is the patient.
- Finishing the customer's sentences or guessing the product.
- Ignoring a customer who waits while you finish a conversation.

## Scenario

On a Thursday afternoon a customer in his fifties asks, "Give me the strongest painkiller for my back." A queue has formed behind him.

**Model approach.** The pharmacist greets him, asks him to describe the pain (open), how long, where it spreads, whether he has numbness, weakness in the legs, fever, difficulty passing urine, or any recent injury (closed red-flag checks), what he has taken, and what other medicines he uses, including for stomach, blood pressure or kidney problems. He answers that the pain started three days ago after lifting a box, there are no alarming signs, and he takes a blood-pressure tablet. The pharmacist summarises, then recommends a suitable option with clear instructions and cautions and advises seeing a doctor if the pain worsens, spreads, or lasts beyond a few days. If he had mentioned leg weakness or urinary problems, the pharmacist would have referred him at once. Meanwhile the pharmacist acknowledged the queue with a quick "one more minute and I am with you" to those waiting.

## Key takeaways

- A warm greeting in the first ten seconds sets the tone, even in a crowded branch.
- Even product-led requests deserve a quick check: who is it for and what is it for.
- Ask open questions first, then closed questions to confirm details and safety.
- Use a simple frame (who, how long, action taken, medicines and conditions) to avoid forgetting key questions.
- Listen without interrupting, reflect back, and confirm understanding before recommending.
- Red flags end the sale and start a referral, calmly and clearly.
