## 1. Mechanism

Sulfonylureas (SUs) bind to receptors on the pancreatic beta cell and close ATP-sensitive potassium (**K-ATP**) channels. The cell depolarises, calcium enters and insulin is released. Because this happens **independent of the blood glucose level**, insulin is released even when glucose is already normal or low — the reason for the main risk of this class: **hypoglycaemia**. SUs need functioning beta cells, so they work best earlier in type 2 diabetes. They are effective, inexpensive and widely used in Egypt, often as an add-on to metformin. Weight gain is a common side effect.

## 2. Gliclazide versus glibenclamide in the elderly

Glibenclamide (also called glyburide) is long acting and has active metabolites that are cleared by the kidneys. In elderly people, who often have reduced kidney function, irregular meals and other medicines, these metabolites accumulate and can cause **prolonged, recurrent hypoglycaemia**. Gliclazide MR (Diamicron MR) has a lower risk of hypoglycaemia and is generally preferred when an SU is needed in older adults or people with mild to moderate kidney impairment. Glimepiride (Amaryl) is a convenient once-daily option. Switching between agents is the physician's decision; the pharmacist's role is to recognise the risk and communicate it.

## 3. Counselling on meals, timing and alcohol

- Take the SU **before or with the first main meal** as directed (Diamicron MR and Amaryl are usually once daily with breakfast). Never take the dose and then skip the meal.
- Eat regularly; do not skip meals, especially during long working days or when fasting.
- Alcohol increases and prolongs hypoglycaemia and can hide its warning signs; avoid it.
- Unplanned heavy exercise, vomiting or reduced food intake increase risk — the patient should check glucose and contact the doctor.
- People who wish to fast (for example during Ramadan) should see the doctor in advance, as SU doses or timing often need adjustment.
- Check for interacting medicines and new prescriptions that may increase hypoglycaemia, and refer if unsure.

## 4. Hypoglycemia management — Rule of 15

Hypoglycaemia is a blood glucose below **70 mg/dL**. Warning signs include sweating, shaking, palpitations, intense hunger, pallor, anxiety, headache, blurred vision and, as it worsens, confusion, unusual behaviour, drowsiness, seizures or loss of consciousness. Elderly people may present mainly with confusion.

1. BG <70 mg/dL (or symptoms when testing is not possible) → if the person is **conscious and able to swallow safely**, give **15 g fast carbohydrate**: about half a cup (120–150 mL) of juice or regular soft drink, one tablespoon of honey or sugar, or glucose tablets totalling 15 g (check the label; often 3–4 tablets).
2. **Wait 15 min** → recheck glucose.
3. Repeat if still below 70.
4. Once stable, eat a meal or snack containing starch.

**Severe hypoglycaemia** (unable to swallow, unconscious, seizure): do not put food or drink in the mouth; give glucagon if available and trained, and call emergency services. Hypoglycaemia caused by SUs can **recur over many hours**, so a person who had a significant episode needs medical assessment, and the SU regimen must be reviewed by the doctor.

**Counseling script (Arabic):**
> "السلفونيل يوريا بينزل السكر بقوة — لازم تاكل بانتظام وماتفوتش وجبة. لو حسيت عرق بارد، رعشة، جوع شديد — قيس السكر فوراً وكل عسل أو عصير. الكحول خطر مع الدوا ده."

## Scenario

> *Elderly patient on glibenclamide 5mg BD found confused at pharmacy. Pale, sweating.*

**Model approach.** The pharmacist treats this as hypoglycaemia until proven otherwise. A colleague checks capillary glucose if possible, but treatment is not delayed. If the patient is conscious and can swallow safely, the pharmacist gives 15 g of fast-acting glucose (juice, sugar in water or glucose tablets), stays with the patient, rechecks after 15 minutes and repeats if needed. If the patient becomes drowsy, cannot swallow or does not improve, the pharmacist calls emergency services immediately. Because glibenclamide hypoglycaemia can recur, the patient should not go home alone; a family member is contacted and the patient is advised to see a doctor the same day. The pharmacist communicates with the physician recommending review of glibenclamide — for example, switching to gliclazide MR — and documents the event per the Lotus SOP.

## Key takeaways

- Sulfonylureas stimulate insulin secretion via K-ATP channels, independent of glucose — hence hypoglycaemia risk.
- Diamicron MR (gliclazide) is the preferred SU in Egypt for the elderly; glibenclamide carries a high hypoglycaemia risk.
- Rule of 15: 15 g fast carbohydrate, wait 15 minutes, recheck, repeat, then eat.
- Take before or with the meal as directed — never skip meals.
- Amaryl (glimepiride) is a once-daily option.
- Alcohol potentiates hypoglycaemia; severe episodes need glucagon or emergency services.
