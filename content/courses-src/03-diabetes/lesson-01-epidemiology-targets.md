## 1. Epidemiology

Diabetes is one of the most common chronic conditions seen at the Egyptian pharmacy counter. Almost every Lotus branch dispenses metformin, sulfonylureas and insulin every day, and many customers buying something else (a painkiller, a vitamin, a cosmetic) have diabetes or are at risk of it.

| Metric | Egypt data |
|---|---|
| Adult diabetes prevalence | **15.6%** (IDF Diabetes Atlas estimate) |
| Undiagnosed | ~50% estimated |
| Prediabetes | Rising with obesity epidemic |
| Risk factors | Obesity, urbanization, sedentary lifestyle, high carb diet |

A prevalence of about 15.6% means that roughly one adult in every six or seven has diabetes. Because about half of people with diabetes are estimated to be undiagnosed, many people in the community have high blood glucose without knowing it. They often feel well for years while damage to the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and blood vessels develops silently.

The main risk factors in Egypt are linked to modern lifestyle: excess body weight (especially around the waist), little physical activity, urban living, and a diet rich in refined carbohydrates such as white bread, rice, pasta, sweets and sugary drinks. Other common risk factors include a family history of diabetes, previous gestational diabetes, high blood pressure, abnormal lipids and increasing age. **Prediabetes** (glucose higher than normal but below the diabetes threshold) is rising with obesity and is the stage where lifestyle change has the greatest chance of preventing or delaying diabetes.

## 2. Treatment targets

Glycated haemoglobin (**HbA1c**, "السكر التراكمي") reflects average blood glucose over about the previous three months. International guidance (ADA/EASD) and Egyptian practice use similar targets, always individualised by the treating physician.

| Patient | HbA1c target | BP | LDL |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most adults | <7% (53 mmol/mol) | <130/80 | <70 mg/dL high risk |
| Elderly/frail | <8% individualized | <140/90 | Individualized |
| Pregnancy (pre-existing) | <6.5% preconception | — | — |

Why individualise? A tight target lowers the risk of long-term complications, but if it is reached with medicines that cause hypoglycaemia it can be dangerous for an elderly person who lives alone, has kidney disease or has had falls. That is why the elderly or frail target is more relaxed. For a woman with pre-existing diabetes who is planning a pregnancy, tighter control before conception protects the baby, and she needs specialist care before and during pregnancy.

## 3. Cardiovascular risk (ASCVD)

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) — heart attack, stroke and peripheral artery disease — is the main cause of death in people with type 2 diabetes. Many Egyptian patients with diabetes also have high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, obesity or smoke. Diabetes care is therefore not only about glucose: blood pressure, lipids, smoking cessation and kidney protection matter just as much. This is why the targets table includes BP and LDL, and why the physician may prescribe a statin or a blood-pressure medicine even when glucose is controlled. In patients with established ASCVD, the physician may set an even lower LDL target.

## 4. Stepwise therapy (Type 2)

The general sequence of type 2 diabetes treatment is:

1. **Lifestyle** — the foundation at every step: diet, weight loss, physical activity, stopping smoking.
2. **Metformin** — first line unless contraindicated or not tolerated.
3. **Add SGLT2i or GLP-1 RA** if the patient has ASCVD, heart failure (HF) or chronic kidney disease (CKD), because these classes protect the heart and kidneys beyond glucose lowering.
4. **Sulfonylurea or DPP-4i** — common, affordable add-ons.
5. **Insulin** — when other therapy is not enough or in specific situations.

Choosing and changing these medicines is the physician's decision. The pharmacist's role is to check, counsel, support adherence and refer.

## 5. Screening and referral from the pharmacy

The pharmacy is often the first health contact. Refer for medical assessment and laboratory testing when a customer:

- has classic symptoms: excessive thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds or recurrent infections;
- has risk factors (overweight, family history, previous gestational diabetes, high blood pressure) and has never been tested;
- has a random glucose reading at a pharmacy screening that is clearly high (follow the Lotus SOP for screening procedures and thresholds).

**Urgent red flags** — refer immediately to emergency care: vomiting with very high glucose, drowsiness or confusion, deep fast breathing, or signs of severe dehydration. A finger-prick reading is a screening tool, not a diagnosis; never tell a customer "you have diabetes" based on one reading.

## 6. Lifestyle-first counselling

Lifestyle advice should be simple, specific and respectful: regular walking (for example, 30 minutes on most days if the doctor agrees), smaller portions of bread and rice, fewer sweets and sugary drinks, more vegetables, weight loss when overweight, and no smoking.

**Counseling script (Arabic):**
> "السكري في مصر شائع جداً — تقريبًا واحد من كل 6 أو 7 بالغين. الهدف إن السكر التراكمي يبقى تحت 7% لو أمكن، والدكتور هو اللي يحدد الهدف المناسب ليك. المشي والدايت هما الأساس مع الدوا. قيس السكر في البيت وسجل الأرقام."

## Scenario

> *Patient HbA1c 8.5% on metformin 1g BD only. BMI 32. Asks pharmacy for 'stronger sugar pill'.*

**Model approach.** The pharmacist explains that an HbA1c of 8.5% is above the usual target of 7%, so the treatment plan needs review — and that adding or changing a medicine is the doctor's decision. The patient is not sold a sulfonylurea or any prescription medicine without a prescription. The pharmacist checks adherence (is metformin taken twice daily with meals?), asks about tolerance, and gives practical diet and walking advice. The patient is referred to the physician with a note of the HbA1c; the pharmacist mentions that, if the patient has heart disease or high cardiovascular risk, the doctor may consider an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist as an add-on.

## Key takeaways

- Egypt diabetes prevalence is about 15.6% in IDF estimates, and around half of cases may be undiagnosed.
- HbA1c target is <7% for most adults, more relaxed (<8%) in elderly or frail patients, and set individually by the physician.
- Metformin remains first-line unless contraindicated.
- SGLT2i/GLP-1 RA are preferred add-ons with ASCVD, HF or CKD.
- Lifestyle modification is the foundation at every step.
- The pharmacy is a screening and referral opportunity (BP and glucose), not a place to diagnose.
