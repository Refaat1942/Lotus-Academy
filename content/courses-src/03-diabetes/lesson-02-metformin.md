## 1. Mechanism & evidence

Metformin is a biguanide and the first-line medicine for most people with type 2 diabetes. It works mainly in the liver: through effects that include activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (**AMPK**), it ↓ hepatic gluconeogenesis (the liver releases less glucose, especially overnight, which lowers fasting glucose) and ↑ insulin sensitivity in muscle. It also has effects in the gut.

Because metformin does not stimulate insulin secretion, it does **not** cause hypoglycaemia when used alone. It is weight neutral or may help modest weight loss, it is inexpensive and widely available in Egypt, and it has decades of experience behind it. In the UKPDS study, metformin reduced cardiovascular events in overweight/obese people with type 2 diabetes. For these reasons it remains the foundation of most regimens unless contraindicated or not tolerated.

## 2. Egyptian market

Many brands contain the same active ingredient, metformin hydrochloride. Customers often do not realise that a "new" brand from a second doctor is the same medicine they already take.

| Brand | Manufacturer | Formulations | Approx. EGP/month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucophage | Merck / generics | 500, 850, 1000mg tabs; XR | 40–120 |
| Cidophage | EIPICO | 500, 850mg | 30–80 |
| Formit | Pharco | 500, 850mg | 25–70 |
| Gliformin | Memphis | 500, 850mg | 30–75 |

Prices are approximate and change frequently; always check the current price in the system. Metformin is also found in fixed-dose combinations with DPP-4 inhibitors or SGLT2 inhibitors (covered in Lesson 3.4) — always check combination products for duplicated metformin.

**Dosing basics.** Treatment usually starts at 500 mg once or twice daily with meals and is increased gradually over weeks ("start low, go slow"). The usual maximum is about 2000–2550 mg/day for immediate-release tablets, as stated in the product leaflet; extended-release (XR) products are usually taken once daily with the evening meal, up to about 2000 mg/day.

## 3. Renal dosing

Metformin is cleared by the kidneys. When kidney function falls, metformin can accumulate and increase the risk of **lactic acidosis**, a rare but serious complication.

| eGFR | Action |
|---|---|
| >45 | Full dose |
| 30–45 | Max 1000mg/day; monitor (initiation generally not recommended) |
| <30 | **Contraindicated** — lactic acidosis risk |

Kidney function (eGFR) should be checked at least yearly, and more often in elderly patients and those with reduced eGFR. When a refill shows a high dose and the patient mentions kidney problems, ask about the latest eGFR result.

**Contraindications:** eGFR <30, acute metabolic acidosis (including diabetic ketoacidosis), severe hepatic impairment, contrast (temporary hold). Caution also applies in situations that reduce oxygen supply or kidney perfusion: severe dehydration, serious infection or shock, acute heart failure and heavy alcohol use.

## 4. Side effects and counselling

**GI side effects** — nausea, abdominal discomfort, diarrhoea, metallic taste — are the most common reason patients stop metformin. They are usually worst in the first weeks and can be reduced by taking metformin **with or after food**, starting at a low dose and titrating slowly, and switching to an **XR formulation**, which is generally better tolerated.

**Vitamin B12.** Long-term metformin can lower vitamin B12 levels. A patient on metformin for years who reports numbness, tingling or unusual tiredness should be referred to the doctor, who may check B12.

**Lactic acidosis red flags** (rare): severe weakness, muscle pain, abdominal pain with vomiting, difficult or fast breathing, unusual drowsiness. Advise urgent medical care.

**Sick days.** If the patient has vomiting, diarrhoea or cannot drink fluids, advise them to contact the doctor; temporary holding of metformin is commonly advised during dehydrating illness.

## 5. Contrast media and surgery

Before iodinated contrast (some CT scans and angiography) or major surgery, metformin may need a temporary hold according to the hospital protocol — typically stopping at the time of or before the procedure and restarting after about 48 hours once kidney function is confirmed stable. The decision belongs to the treating physician or radiology team; the pharmacist's job is to make sure the patient tells them about metformin.

**Counseling script (Arabic):**
> "الميتفورمين يتاخد مع الأكل عشان يقلل عوارض المعدة. ابدأ بجرعة صغيرة وزود تدريجياً. لو عندك أشعة بصبغة أو عملية، قول للدكتور — ممكن يوقف الدوا يومين."

## Scenario

> *Patient eGFR 28 on metformin 850mg TDS. Refill request.*

**Model approach.** An eGFR of 28 is below 30, where metformin is contraindicated, and 2550 mg/day is the maximum dose — a combination with a real risk of accumulation and lactic acidosis. The pharmacist does not refill this regimen without physician adjustment. The pharmacist explains calmly to the patient why the kidney result matters, contacts or refers to the prescribing physician urgently for a regimen change (following the Lotus SOP for prescriber contact), documents the intervention, and reminds the patient not to stop all diabetes treatment on their own but to see the doctor today or as soon as possible.

## Key takeaways

- Glucophage, Cidophage, Formit and Gliformin all contain metformin; check for duplicate therapy.
- Metformin is contraindicated when eGFR is <30, with a dose limit at 30–45.
- Start low, go slow, and take with food for GI tolerance; XR formulations reduce GI side effects.
- Hold before iodinated contrast or major surgery per protocol.
- Metformin alone does not cause hypoglycaemia; long-term use can lower vitamin B12.
- UKPDS showed CV benefit in obese T2DM.
