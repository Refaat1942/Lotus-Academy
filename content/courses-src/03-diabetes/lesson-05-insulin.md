## 1. Insulin types

Insulin is essential for everyone with type 1 diabetes and is used in type 2 diabetes when other medicines are not enough, during pregnancy, and in some acute situations. Insulins differ mainly in how quickly they start working and how long they last.

| Type | Onset | Duration | Egyptian brands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid | 15 min | 3–5h | Humalog, NovoRapid, APIDRA |
| Short | 30 min | 6–8h | Actrapid |
| Intermediate | 1–2h | 12–18h | Insulatard |
| Basal analog | 1–2h | ~24h | Lantus, Levemir, Tresiba |
| Premixed | Variable | 12–24h | Mixtard 30, NovoMix 30, Humalog Mix |

Times are approximate and vary between products and patients; Tresiba (degludec) lasts longer than 24 hours. In practice:

- **Rapid-acting analogues** (lispro, aspart, glulisine) are injected just before meals (usually 0–15 minutes) to cover the meal.
- **Short-acting (regular) insulin** such as Actrapid is injected about 30 minutes before meals.
- **Intermediate (NPH)** insulin such as Insulatard is cloudy and must be mixed gently before use.
- **Basal analogues** such as Lantus (glargine) are given once daily at the same time each day to control fasting glucose.
- **Premixed insulins** combine a short or rapid component with an intermediate one (for example 30/70) and are usually injected twice daily before breakfast and dinner. They are common in Egypt because of their simplicity, but they require regular meals.

## 2. Egyptian market

| Brand | Type | Manufacturer | Approx. EGP/month |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lantus | Glargine U100 | Sanofi | 400–800 |
| Humalog | Lispro | Lilly | 500–900 |
| Mixtard 30 | 30/70 | Novo Nordisk | 200–400 |
| Basaglar | Glargine biosimilar | Lilly | 350–700 |
| Local biosimilars | Glargine/aspart | Egyptian/import | 200–500 |

Prices are approximate and change frequently. **Biosimilars** are reducing cost in the Egyptian market. A switch between insulin brands — including to a biosimilar — should be made by the prescriber, with closer glucose monitoring afterwards; never substitute one insulin for another at the counter without the prescriber's agreement. Always double-check the **name, type and concentration** (for example U100 versus higher concentrations) on every insulin dispensed, as mix-ups between rapid and basal insulin are a classic serious error.

## 3. Pen versus vial

| | Pen | Vial and syringe |
|---|---|---|
| Ease of use | Easier, dial the dose | Needs drawing up skill |
| Dose accuracy | High, good for low vision or elderly | Depends on technique |
| Cost | Usually higher | Usually lower |
| Notes | Needles bought separately; never share | Use insulin syringes matching the concentration (U100) |

## 4. Storage

**Storage:** 2–8°C refrigerator; in-use room temp <30°C × 28–42 days. Never freeze.

- Store unopened insulin in the main part of the refrigerator, away from the freezer compartment and not touching the cooling plate at the back.
- In-use pens or vials can usually be kept at room temperature below about 30 °C for the period in the leaflet (commonly 28 days; some products longer). Write the opening date on the pen.
- In Egyptian summers, keep insulin away from direct sun, hot cars and windowsills; use an insulated bag when travelling.
- Discard insulin that has been frozen, overheated, is past its in-use period, or looks abnormal (clear insulin that becomes cloudy, or clumps in cloudy insulin).

## 5. Injection technique and rotation

- Wash hands; for cloudy insulin, roll and tip gently (do not shake) until evenly mixed.
- Prime the pen (usually 2 units) before each injection to check flow.
- Inject subcutaneously into the abdomen, thighs, buttocks or upper arms. Hold for about 10 seconds before withdrawing the needle.
- **Rotate injection sites** within an area and between areas. Repeated injections in one spot cause **lipodystrophy** (lumpy fatty tissue), which makes absorption unpredictable.
- Use a new needle each time and dispose of sharps safely in a hard container.

## 6. Hypoglycaemia and sick-day rules

Hypoglycaemia (glucose below 70 mg/dL) is the main risk of insulin. Treat with **15 g fast glucose** using the rule of 15 (Lesson 3.3), and refer severe episodes to emergency care. Common causes are a missed meal, too much insulin, extra exercise or alcohol.

**Sick-day rules:** never stop insulin when ill. Illness and infection often raise glucose, so the patient may need **more** insulin, not less. Check glucose more often (for example every 3–4 hours), drink fluids, keep taking carbohydrate in an easy form if not eating normally, and check ketones if advised (especially type 1). Contact the doctor early, and seek urgent care for vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, drowsiness or rapid breathing.

**Counseling script (Arabic):**
> "الأنسولين مايتجمّدش أبداً. خزنه في الثلاجة — مش الفريزر. غيّر مكان الحقن كل مرة. لو السكر نزل تحت 70، خد عسل أو عصير فوراً. لما تكون مريض، قيس السكر أكتر وكلم الدكتور."

## Scenario

> *Patient storing insulin in freezer compartment by mistake. Insulin frozen.*

**Model approach.** The pharmacist explains that frozen insulin must be discarded, even after it thaws, because freezing destroys its potency and using it can lead to high glucose. The patient needs a new supply (according to the prescription and the Lotus SOP). The pharmacist then counsels on correct storage: unopened insulin in the main compartment of the refrigerator (the door shelf or a middle shelf is acceptable), never in or next to the freezer and not against the back cooling plate; the pen in use at room temperature away from heat and sun. Because the patient may have injected partly inactive insulin, the pharmacist advises checking glucose more often for the next few days and contacting the doctor if readings are high.

## Key takeaways

- Lantus (glargine) and Humalog (lispro) are the main branded insulins in Egypt; biosimilars such as Basaglar are reducing cost.
- Know the profile: rapid, short, intermediate, basal and premixed insulin differ in onset and duration.
- Never freeze insulin; store unopened at 2–8 °C and in-use at room temperature for the period in the leaflet.
- Injection site rotation prevents lipodystrophy.
- Sick-day rules: never stop insulin when ill — more insulin may be needed.
- Premixed insulin is common for simplicity but needs regular meals; always double-check insulin type at dispensing.
