# Final Exam — Diabetes Medications in Egypt

## Final Exam (20 questions)

**Q1.** A frail 82-year-old who lives alone and has had two falls has an HbA1c of 7.6%. Her son asks whether she is "out of control". What is the most appropriate response?
- a) Yes; she must reach below 6% as soon as possible
- b) Yes; buy a stronger tablet today without a prescription
- c) A more relaxed target (often below 8%) is usually appropriate for frail elderly people; the doctor sets her individual target ✓
- d) HbA1c is not relevant for elderly people

**Q2.** A customer with obesity, a family history of diabetes and frequent thirst and urination has never been tested. A random finger-prick reading at the pharmacy is clearly high. What do you do?
- a) Explain that the reading needs medical assessment and laboratory confirmation, and refer him to the doctor ✓
- b) Tell him he has diabetes and sell him metformin
- c) Tell him the reading is meaningless and ignore it
- d) Sell him a meter and advise him to come back in a year

**Q3.** A patient with type 2 diabetes and a previous heart attack is on metformin only with HbA1c 8.2%. Which add-on classes are generally preferred for his heart protection, as decided by the doctor?
- a) Sulfonylureas only
- b) Short-acting insulin before every meal
- c) Vitamin supplements
- d) An SGLT2 inhibitor or a GLP-1 receptor agonist ✓

**Q4.** A patient's lab report shows eGFR 38. He has been on metformin 1000 mg twice daily for years. What is the most appropriate action?
- a) No action; any dose is fine above an eGFR of 30
- b) Refer to the physician, because at eGFR 30–45 the usual maximum is about 1000 mg/day and review is needed ✓
- c) Stop all his diabetes medicines immediately
- d) Increase the dose to protect the kidneys

**Q5.** A patient stopped Glucophage after one week because of diarrhoea and nausea. What is the best advice to share with him and his doctor?
- a) Taking it with food, slower dose increase, or an XR formulation can improve tolerance; discuss with the doctor before restarting ✓
- b) Metformin side effects never improve, so he should never take it again
- c) Take it on an empty stomach to absorb it faster
- d) Replace it with a herbal tea

**Q6.** A long-term metformin user complains of tingling and numbness in his feet and unusual tiredness. What should the pharmacist consider?
- a) It is certainly a side effect of his shoes only
- b) Advise him to double the metformin dose
- c) Possible vitamin B12 deficiency (or neuropathy); refer to the doctor for assessment ✓
- d) Nothing; metformin has no long-term effects

**Q7.** An elderly patient on glibenclamide reports sweating and shaking most afternoons when lunch is late. What is the best pharmacist action?
- a) Advise her to skip lunch to lose weight
- b) Explain hypoglycaemia and the rule of 15, advise regular meals, and communicate with the doctor about reviewing glibenclamide (for example to gliclazide MR) ✓
- c) Tell her to take an extra tablet in the afternoon
- d) Reassure her that sulfonylureas never cause low sugar

**Q8.** A patient on Amaryl had a hypoglycaemia episode, took juice and recovered. He plans to drive home immediately. What is the safest advice?
- a) Drive straight away because the juice works for the whole day
- b) Take another Amaryl tablet before driving
- c) Drink alcohol to stay awake
- d) Recheck after 15 minutes, eat a starchy snack, and do not drive until glucose has recovered; hypoglycaemia from sulfonylureas can recur ✓

**Q9.** A patient asks whether he can keep his usual morning Diamicron MR dose during Ramadan fasting. What is the correct approach?
- a) Take it as usual in the morning while fasting
- b) Double the dose at sunset
- c) Advise him to see his doctor before Ramadan, as the dose or timing often needs adjustment ✓
- d) Stop all diabetes medicines during the month

**Q10.** A patient on Forxiga is admitted for planned major surgery next week. What should he be reminded about?
- a) Tell the surgical team he takes an SGLT2 inhibitor, as it may need to be paused before surgery ✓
- b) Double the dose the night before surgery
- c) SGLT2 inhibitors never matter in surgery
- d) Stop drinking all fluids for three days before surgery

**Q11.** A woman on Jardiance has nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and fast breathing, but her meter reads only 180 mg/dL. What should you advise?
- a) Reassure her because her glucose is not very high
- b) Take an extra Jardiance dose
- c) Treat at home with antacids
- d) Seek urgent medical care; this may be ketoacidosis, which can occur with near-normal glucose on SGLT2 inhibitors ✓

**Q12.** A customer without a prescription asks to buy Ozempic "to lose a few kilos before a wedding". What is the most appropriate response?
- a) Sell it because it is popular for weight loss
- b) Explain politely that Ozempic is a prescription medicine to be used under a doctor's supervision, and refer her for medical advice ✓
- c) Sell one pen only as a trial
- d) Recommend she borrows a friend's pen

**Q13.** A patient on metformin is prescribed Januvia as an add-on. He worries about low sugar. What is accurate?
- a) Januvia always causes severe hypoglycaemia
- b) DPP-4 inhibitors alone rarely cause hypoglycaemia because their effect depends on glucose being raised; report severe persistent abdominal pain ✓
- c) Januvia must be injected weekly
- d) Januvia works by making glucose leave in the urine

**Q14.** A patient brings a prescription for Lantus, but the technician has picked Humalog from the refrigerator. What is the risk?
- a) No risk; all insulins are interchangeable
- b) Only a price difference
- c) Humalog lasts longer than Lantus, so the patient would be undertreated
- d) A serious error: rapid-acting insulin given as a once-daily basal dose can cause severe hypoglycaemia; always double-check type and name ✓

**Q15.** A patient on Mixtard 30 asks how to prepare the cloudy pen before injecting. What is correct?
- a) Roll and tip it gently until evenly mixed, prime, then inject subcutaneously ✓
- b) Shake it hard for one minute
- c) Warm it in boiling water
- d) Inject it without mixing

**Q16.** In July, a patient tells you he keeps his in-use insulin pen on the car dashboard. What advice do you give?
- a) This is fine because insulin likes heat
- b) Keep the in-use pen in the freezer
- c) Keep it away from heat and sun, below about 30 °C, and use an insulated bag when out; discard insulin that has overheated ✓
- d) Leave it in the car but cover it with paper

**Q17.** A patient's meter readings are consistently much higher than his HbA1c suggests. He tests right after peeling oranges and keeps strips in an open vial in the bathroom. What is the most likely explanation and advice?
- a) His meter proves he needs insulin today
- b) His HbA1c result must be wrong
- c) He should switch to testing only once a year
- d) Poor technique and strip storage; wash and dry hands before testing and keep strips in the dry, capped vial away from humidity ✓

**Q18.** A patient with diabetes shows you a small painless blister on his sole that he noticed by chance. What is the best advice?
- a) Pop it at home and cover it with a corn plaster
- b) Refer him for prompt medical review, keep it clean and covered, and avoid walking barefoot ✓
- c) Ignore it because it is not painful
- d) Soak the foot in very hot water for an hour

**Q19.** A patient on intensive insulin has frequent night-time lows that he does not feel. Which monitoring option is most useful, if he can afford it?
- a) Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), discussed with his doctor ✓
- b) Checking HbA1c every week
- c) Testing urine glucose once a day
- d) Stopping all monitoring at night

**Q20.** A patient on metformin, Diamicron MR and atorvastatin asks what yearly checks he needs besides HbA1c. What is the most complete answer?
- a) Only a chest X-ray
- b) Only a hearing test
- c) Eye examination, foot examination, urine albumin, kidney function and lipids ✓
