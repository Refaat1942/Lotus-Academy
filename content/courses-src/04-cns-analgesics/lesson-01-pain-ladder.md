## 1. Pain types: nociceptive and neuropathic

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience. It is always real to the patient, even when there is no visible injury. For counter practice, two main types matter, because they respond to different medicines.

**Nociceptive pain** comes from tissue injury or inflammation: a sprained ankle, toothache, period pain, osteoarthritis, pain after surgery, muscle-tension headache. Nerve endings called nociceptors detect the damage and send signals through the spinal cord to the brain. Inflammatory chemicals such as prostaglandins make these nerve endings more sensitive, which is why paracetamol and NSAIDs usually help. Patients describe it as aching, throbbing or sharp, and can usually point to where it hurts.

**Neuropathic pain** comes from damage or disease of the nerves themselves: diabetic neuropathy, pain after shingles (post-herpetic neuralgia), sciatica from a compressed nerve root, trigeminal neuralgia. Patients describe it as **burning, shooting, stabbing or electric**, often with pins and needles, numbness, or pain from a light touch such as clothes or bed sheets. Neuropathic pain responds poorly to simple analgesics and usually needs adjuvant medicines that a doctor prescribes and adjusts.

| Feature | Nociceptive | Neuropathic |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Tissue injury or inflammation | Nerve damage or disease |
| Description | Aching, throbbing, sharp | Burning, shooting, electric, tingling |
| Typical examples | Sprain, toothache, arthritis, period pain | Diabetic feet, post-shingles pain, sciatica |
| Response to paracetamol/NSAIDs | Usually good | Usually poor |
| Main extra treatment | Rest, ice/heat, physiotherapy | Adjuvants prescribed by a doctor |

Many patients have **mixed pain**, for example low back pain with sciatica down one leg. Pain is also described by duration: **acute pain** is a warning signal that usually settles as the tissue heals, while pain lasting more than about three months is usually called **chronic** and needs medical follow-up rather than repeated self-treatment.

## 2. The WHO analgesic ladder

The World Health Organization ladder was first developed for cancer pain, but its logic guides stepwise analgesia in general: choose the step that matches the severity of the pain, move up if pain is not controlled, and step down when it improves.

| Step | Drugs (Egypt) |
|---|---|
| 1 Mild | Cetal, Brufen |
| 2 Moderate | + Tramal (tramadol) |
| 3 Severe | Morphine (hospital) |
| Adjuvant | Amitriptyline, Lyrica |

- **Step 1 — mild pain:** a non-opioid such as paracetamol (Cetal) or an NSAID such as ibuprofen (Brufen), with or without an adjuvant. These are the medicines most often supplied over the counter.
- **Step 2 — moderate pain:** a **weak opioid** such as tramadol (Tramal) or codeine is **added** to the non-opioid. In Egypt these are prescription and controlled medicines.
- **Step 3 — severe pain:** a **strong opioid** such as morphine, usually started and supervised in hospital or by a specialist, for example in severe cancer pain.
- **Adjuvants** at any step: medicines that are not classic painkillers but help certain pain types, especially neuropathic pain — amitriptyline, gabapentin, pregabalin (Lyrica) and duloxetine. All are prescription medicines; pregabalin and gabapentin also have misuse potential (see Lesson 4.8).

The WHO principles are often summarised as: by mouth where possible, **by the clock** (regular doses for persistent pain rather than waiting for pain to return), by the ladder, for the individual, and with attention to detail. Steps 2 and 3 are never started at the counter. Dispensing of controlled analgesics must follow the current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority / Ministry of Health and the Lotus SOP.

## 3. Red-flag pain: when to refer

The pharmacist counsels and does not diagnose. Some pain patterns must never be treated with a painkiller and a smile; they need urgent medical assessment:

- **Thunderclap headache:** sudden, severe, "worst ever" headache that peaks within seconds to minutes — emergency referral immediately.
- Headache with fever, stiff neck, rash, confusion, weakness of one side, speech or vision problems, or after a head injury.
- Chest pain, especially spreading to the arm, jaw or back, or with sweating or breathlessness.
- Severe abdominal pain, a rigid abdomen, vomiting blood or black stools.
- Back pain with leg weakness, numbness around the genitals or buttocks, or loss of bladder or bowel control; or back pain with fever, weight loss or a history of cancer.
- Pain in pregnancy with bleeding, or any severe pain in a young child or a frail elderly person.
- Pain that has not improved after a few days of correct OTC use, or that keeps coming back.

## 4. Counselling on pain expectations

Patients often expect pain to disappear completely and quickly. Good counselling sets realistic goals: reduce pain to a tolerable level and restore function, such as sleeping, walking or working. Explain how long the medicine takes to act, the maximum daily dose, and that the same active ingredient can hide in several products (for example paracetamol in cold and flu remedies).

**Counseling (Arabic):** "الألم العصبي محتاج أدوية مختلفة. متزودش الجرعة من نفسك — اتبع الدكتور."

Customer: "The doctor gave my father a painkiller, but it is not enough. Can I give him two tablets instead of one?"
Pharmacist: "Please do not increase it yourself. Some painkillers, especially the strong ones, become dangerous at higher doses. Let us write down when the pain comes and how strong it is, and share that with the doctor so the dose can be adjusted safely."

A **pain diary** helps track treatment response. Ask the patient to note the time, a pain score from 0 to 10, the dose taken, what activity triggered the pain, and any side effects. The doctor can then adjust treatment based on facts rather than memory.

## Scenario

> Thunderclap headache — refer emergency immediately.

A woman in her forties arrives at a busy branch late in the evening with her husband. She asks for "the strongest painkiller you have". While lifting a heavy box an hour ago, she felt a sudden, explosive headache, the worst of her life, and she feels nauseated.

**Model approach:** The pharmacist does not sell an analgesic. This is a red-flag thunderclap headache. The pharmacist calmly explains that this type of headache must be examined in an emergency department now, advises the husband to take her immediately (or calls an ambulance according to the Lotus SOP if she looks unwell), and avoids giving painkillers that could delay assessment. The pharmacist records the advice given.

A second customer, a man with long-standing diabetes, asks for Brufen for "burning feet at night". The pharmacist recognises a neuropathic description, explains that ordinary painkillers rarely help this kind of pain, advises checking the feet daily for wounds, and refers him to his doctor to review his diabetes and discuss suitable treatment.

## Key takeaways

- The WHO ladder guides stepwise analgesia: non-opioid, then add a weak opioid, then a strong opioid, with adjuvants at any step.
- Neuropathic pain (burning, shooting) needs adjuvants prescribed by a doctor; simple analgesics rarely work alone.
- Red-flag pain, such as a thunderclap headache, needs urgent referral, not a stronger painkiller.
- Tramadol is a step 2 medicine and is controlled in Egypt; follow EDA/MOH rules and the Lotus SOP.
- OTC paracetamol and NSAIDs (Cetal, Brufen) belong to step 1.
- Patients must never self-adjust opioid or adjuvant doses; a pain diary helps the doctor decide.
