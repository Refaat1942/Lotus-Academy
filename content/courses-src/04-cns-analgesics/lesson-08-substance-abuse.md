After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Describe the **scope of prescription-medicine misuse** in Egypt
2. Identify misuse patterns of **tramadol, benzodiazepines and pregabalin**

4. Counsel **families** on warning signs and treatment in Arabic
5. Integrate the **controlled-medicine principles** of Course 4 into daily practice

## 1. The Egyptian context

Egypt has faced a serious problem with the misuse of prescription medicines. In the 2010s, **tramadol misuse** became widespread, especially among young men and workers seeking energy, euphoria or the ability to work long hours. The problem shaped national drug policy and led to tighter controls. Other concerns include **benzodiazepine diversion** (for example Xanax and other tranquillisers obtained without proper prescriptions), and the **emerging misuse of pregabalin (Lyrica)** and gabapentin, often in high doses or combined with opioids or sedatives.

Pharmacies sit at the point where these medicines reach the public. That gives pharmacists a real responsibility and a real opportunity: every correct refusal, every documented suspicious pattern and every kind referral protects someone.

## 2. Recognising misuse

**Medicines most often misused** in this course: tramadol and other opioids, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, pregabalin and gabapentin, and stimulants such as methylphenidate.

**Counter warning signs (red flags):**

- **Doctor shopping:** prescriptions for the same medicine from several doctors or clinics.
- **Early refill** requests, "lost" prescriptions or tablets, and repeated excuses.
- Asking for a specific brand and strength by name and refusing alternatives.
- Prescriptions that look altered, photocopied, unusual in quantity or from a distant clinic.
- **Multiple similar prescriptions** from one clinic for different young patients.
- Signs of intoxication or withdrawal: pinpoint or very large pupils, slurred speech, drowsiness, sweating, restlessness, yawning, aggression.
- Paying cash for large quantities, or trying to buy across several branches.

**The most dangerous combinations** are opioids (including tramadol) with benzodiazepines, pregabalin or gabapentin, and alcohol, because together they can stop breathing.

## 3. The pharmacist's role

- **Verify every prescription** for controlled medicines and dispense only according to the current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority / Ministry of Health and the Lotus SOP.
- **Refuse invalid prescriptions and early refills**, politely and consistently, for every customer. Never accept "just this once".
- **Record** controlled supplies in the **controlled-drug register** as the rules require, and **document suspicious patterns** in line with the SOP.
- **Escalate** to the pharmacy manager; where patterns suggest forged prescriptions or organised diversion, **report according to the regulations** and the SOP.
- **Refer** people who want help to approved **addiction treatment services** (government and Ministry of Health addiction treatment centres or a doctor), without judgement.
- Protect yourself and colleagues: never argue with an aggressive customer, call the manager, and follow the branch safety procedure.

**Counseling (Arabic):** "التعاطي الخاطئ بيدمر الصحة. الصيدلي ملزم مايصرفش بدون روشتة. ساعد اللي حواليك يروح لعلاج الإدمان."

## 4. Talking with families

Families often come to the pharmacist first. Teach them the **warning signs**: changes in sleep and appetite, mood swings, withdrawal from family, falling school or work performance, missing money, unexplained pill strips at home, drowsiness or unusual energy. Advise them to speak calmly, avoid humiliation or threats, store family medicines securely, and seek help from addiction treatment services. Addiction is a treatable condition; treatment is more successful when the family is involved. Any overdose signs — very slow breathing, failure to wake, blue lips, seizures — need emergency services immediately.

## Scenario

> Multiple similar Tramal Rx from one clinic — report per regulations.

Over two weeks, the branch receives several Tramal prescriptions from the same clinic, in the same handwriting, for different young men, each for the maximum quantity. Some customers come together.

**Model approach:** The pharmacist does not dispense prescriptions that appear invalid, checks the prescriptions carefully according to the SOP, and documents the pattern (dates, quantities, prescriber details, without confrontation at the counter). The pharmacist informs the pharmacy manager, who follows the Lotus SOP to report the pattern according to the current EDA/MOH regulations. Customers are told politely that the pharmacy cannot dispense the prescription, without accusations.

## Key takeaways

- The tramadol epidemic shaped Egyptian drug policy and current controls.
- The pharmacist must refuse invalid prescriptions and early refills, every time.
- Pregabalin (Lyrica) misuse is an emerging concern.
- Early refills and doctor shopping are red flags.
- Opioids combined with benzodiazepines, pregabalin or alcohol can be fatal.
- Refer people to approved addiction treatment services, without judgement.
- Course 4 complete: safe analgesia and psychotropic care depend on verification, counselling and referral.
