## 1. Opioid pharmacology

Opioids act on opioid receptors, mainly the **mu receptor**, in the brain, spinal cord and gut. Stimulating these receptors reduces the perception of pain, but it also causes:

- **Respiratory depression** — slowed, shallow breathing. This is the effect that kills in overdose, and the risk rises sharply when opioids are combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, pregabalin, gabapentin or other sedatives.
- Sedation, dizziness and impaired driving.
- **Constipation**, which does not improve with time, and nausea, which often settles after a few days.
- Small (pinpoint) pupils, itching and urinary retention.
- **Tolerance** (the same dose gives less effect), **physical dependence** (withdrawal symptoms if stopped suddenly) and, in some people, **addiction** (compulsive use despite harm).

**Tramadol** is a weak mu agonist (largely through an active metabolite) **plus** an inhibitor of serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake, similar to an SNRI antidepressant. This dual action explains its specific risks: **seizures** (more likely at high doses, in overdose, or in people with epilepsy or other risk factors) and **serotonin syndrome** when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, MAOIs or other serotonergic drugs.

**Codeine** is converted to morphine in the liver by the CYP2D6 enzyme. People who convert it very quickly can get dangerous morphine levels, which is why codeine products are restricted in young children and must be used exactly as the leaflet and prescriber state.

## 2. Opioids in Egypt

| Drug | Brand | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Tramadol | Tramal, Contramal | Controlled Rx |
| Codeine | Codeine compound | Controlled |
| Morphine | MST | Strict controlled |

Morphine (for example the modified-release MST) is a step 3 strong opioid, used mainly for severe pain such as cancer pain under hospital or specialist supervision.

## 3. The tramadol misuse problem and the pharmacist's duties

Egypt experienced widespread misuse of tramadol, often by young people seeking energy, euphoria or "endurance" at work. The Egyptian Drug Authority tightened controls in response. Every dispensing of an opioid must follow the **current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority / Ministry of Health and the Lotus SOP** on prescriptions, quantities, storage and record keeping. In practice, the pharmacist must:

- **Verify the prescription** carefully: a genuine, complete, legible prescription from a prescriber, for this patient, with dose and quantity, not altered.
- **Limit quantities** to what is prescribed and permitted, and record the supply in the controlled-drug register as the SOP requires.
- **Refuse doctor shopping**, early refills and suspicious prescriptions, and escalate to the pharmacy manager.
- **Never dispense without a valid prescription**, whatever the excuse, relationship or pressure.

**Warning signs of misuse at the counter:** asking for a specific brand and strength by name, requests for "extra" or "for travel", early or frequent refills, prescriptions from several doctors, altered numbers, paying cash for large quantities, signs of intoxication, or a story that changes.

## 4. Counselling patients who are prescribed opioids

**Counseling (Arabic, corrected):** "ترامادول محتاج روشتة ومتابعة. ممكن يسبب تعود واعتماد حتى مع الاستخدام الصح، علشان كده خده بالجرعة اللي الدكتور كتبها بالظبط ومتوقفوش فجأة. اشرب مية وكل ألياف للإمساك."

- Take exactly as prescribed; never increase the dose or share tablets.
- **Constipation:** drink plenty of water, eat fibre (vegetables, fruit, bran), keep active, and ask the doctor about a laxative if needed.
- Avoid alcohol, sleeping tablets and other sedatives unless the doctor knows; do not drive if drowsy.
- Tell the doctor and pharmacist about all other medicines, especially antidepressants (risk of serotonin syndrome) and epilepsy medicines.
- **Do not stop suddenly** after regular use; the doctor will reduce the dose gradually.
- Store securely, away from children and other family members.
- **Emergency signs:** very slow or noisy breathing, extreme drowsiness or failure to wake, blue lips, or a seizure — call emergency services immediately.

## Scenario

> Tramal request without Rx — refuse, counsel physician visit, watch for misuse patterns.

A young man asks for "a strip of Tramal, the strong one" without a prescription. He says his back hurts and he has a long shift at work.

**Model approach:**

Pharmacist: "I understand your back is hurting. Tramal can only be dispensed with a valid prescription; that is the law and the rule in our pharmacy, for everyone. For back pain now, I can suggest paracetamol or, if it is suitable for you, a pain-relief gel. If the pain is strong or continues, please see a doctor."

The pharmacist stays calm and respectful, does not lecture, does not accept "just this once", and offers a safe alternative. If the customer returns repeatedly, becomes aggressive or shows signs of intoxication, the pharmacist informs the pharmacy manager and follows the SOP for documenting suspicious requests.

## Key takeaways

- Tramadol is a controlled medicine in Egypt; a valid prescription is mandatory.
- Tramal and Contramal are common tramadol brands.
- The tramadol misuse epidemic shaped Egyptian regulations; follow EDA/MOH rules and the Lotus SOP.
- Respiratory depression is the most dangerous opioid risk, especially with alcohol or other sedatives.
- Constipation counselling is essential for every opioid patient.
- Tramadol plus an SSRI can cause serotonin syndrome; tramadol can also cause seizures.
- Dependence can develop even with prescribed use; never dispense without a valid prescription.
