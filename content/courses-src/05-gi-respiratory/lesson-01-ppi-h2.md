## 1. How acid suppression works

The stomach lining contains parietal cells that secrete hydrochloric acid. The final step of acid secretion is a "pump" in the cell wall called **H+/K+ ATPase** (the proton pump). Several signals switch the pump on, and one of the most important is **histamine** acting on **H2 receptors** on the parietal cell.

- **PPIs** (esomeprazole, pantoprazole, omeprazole, rabeprazole) block the proton pump itself. Because they act on the final common step, they give strong and long-lasting acid suppression. They work best on pumps that are active, which is why they are taken **30–60 minutes before food**, usually before breakfast, so that the drug is in the blood when the meal switches the pumps on. Full effect builds over a few days of regular dosing, so a PPI is not an "instant" heartburn remedy.
- **H2 blockers** (famotidine) block the histamine signal. They act faster than a PPI but suppress acid less strongly, and tolerance can develop with continuous use. They suit mild or occasional symptoms and night-time symptoms.
- **Antacids and alginates** (not the focus of this lesson) neutralise acid already present and give quick, short relief.

Nexium and Controloc are the brands customers ask for most often. Omeprazole generics are an affordable option when the prescriber agrees and the patient needs long courses. Capsules and tablets should be swallowed whole, not crushed or chewed, unless the product leaflet allows it.

## 3. H2 blockers

Famotidine (Pepcid, and the Egyptian brand **Antodine**) is the H2 blocker you will meet most at the counter. Ranitidine has been **withdrawn in many markets** because of an impurity concern, so its availability is limited. Cimetidine is an older H2 blocker with more drug interactions and is rarely used today.

**When an H2 blocker may fit:** mild, infrequent heartburn; night-time symptoms; a patient who prefers a short course. **When a PPI is usually preferred:** frequent or troublesome reflux (GERD), healing of erosive oesophagitis, ulcer treatment and H. pylori regimens, as decided by the doctor.

## 4. Long-term PPI use: risks and review

PPIs are safe for most people in the short term, but long-term use without review is common in Egypt. Long-term risks include:

- **Hypomagnesemia** (low magnesium), which can cause muscle cramps, tremor or an irregular heartbeat
- **C. difficile** infection and other gut infections
- **Fracture** risk with long-term, high-dose use
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency**

The pharmacist's role is not to stop a PPI, but to ask whether the indication has been reviewed recently and to encourage a doctor's review, especially after many months of continuous use. Some patients need long-term therapy (for example, those protecting the stomach while taking certain medicines); the decision belongs to the doctor.

**Counseling (Arabic):** "العلاج 30-60 دقيقة قبل الأكل. لو أعراض رجعوا بعد الإيقاف، راجع الدكتور — ممكن التهاب جرثومة المعدة."

## 5. H. pylori regimen

A common regimen in Egypt is **PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin (Klacid) × 14 days**, prescribed by a doctor. Because clarithromycin resistance is a concern, doctors may choose other combinations; dispense exactly what is prescribed. Counsel the patient to:

- take every dose and complete the full course even if pain improves
- expect possible side effects such as a metallic taste, loose stools or nausea, and call the pharmacy if they are severe
- tell the pharmacist about any penicillin allergy before starting
- follow up with the doctor, who may arrange a test to confirm eradication

## 6. Red flags: refer to a doctor

Refer rather than sell an acid suppressant when the customer reports difficulty or pain on swallowing, vomiting blood or black stools, unintended weight loss, persistent vomiting, chest pain that may be cardiac, new symptoms in an older adult, or symptoms that continue despite two weeks of treatment.

## Scenario

> PPI × 3 years without review — counsel magnesium, B12, reassess indication.

A middle-aged customer at a busy Lotus branch buys Nexium 40 mg every month. On questioning, the customer started it three years ago "for the stomach" and has never been back to the doctor. Lately they have had leg cramps.

**Model approach:** The pharmacist asks about the original reason, current symptoms and red flags. The pharmacist explains that long-term PPIs can lower magnesium and B12, and that cramps should be checked. The pharmacist does not stop the medicine but advises a doctor's review to reassess whether the dose or the medicine itself is still needed, and notes the advice on the customer's record according to the Lotus SOP.

## Key takeaways

- Nexium and Controloc are the dominant PPI brands in Egypt; omeprazole generics are an affordable option
- Take a PPI 30–60 minutes before meals, usually before breakfast
- H. pylori treatment combines a PPI with amoxicillin and clarithromycin (Klacid) or another prescribed regimen, completed in full
- Long-term PPI use needs monitoring and periodic review by a doctor
- Antodine (famotidine) is an H2 blocker suited to mild GERD symptoms
- Refer red flags such as swallowing difficulty, bleeding signs or weight loss
