## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Use LactMed principles to judge whether a medicine is compatible with breastfeeding
2. Identify medicines generally compatible with breastfeeding, such as most penicillins and paracetamol
3. Counsel on medicines that are incompatible or need special handling, such as some chemotherapy and radiopharmaceuticals
4. Support the management of mastitis, including antibiotic selection and continued breastfeeding
5. Advise on timing doses relative to feeds and on what to watch for in the baby

---

## 1. Principles: most medicines are compatible

Breastfeeding has major benefits for mother and baby, and stopping it is rarely necessary because of a medicine. Many mothers in Egypt stop breastfeeding, or stop a needed medicine, because someone told them "all medicines pass into the milk". The pharmacist's role is to give accurate, reassuring and safe advice based on evidence.

How much of a medicine reaches the baby depends on:

- **The baby**: newborns, and especially premature babies, clear medicines more slowly than older infants.
- **The medicine**: long half-life, high oral absorption, low protein binding and small molecular size increase transfer; medicines that are poorly absorbed from the gut reach the baby in tiny amounts.
- **The dose and duration**: a short course carries less concern than long-term high doses.
- **Previous use**: a medicine that is approved for use directly in infants is often reassuring.

**LactMed** (a free database from the US National Library of Medicine) is the reference standard for breastfeeding. Other good sources are the BNF lactation information and the product leaflet. Note that leaflets often say "not recommended" simply because no studies were done, so combine sources and contact the prescriber when unsure.

## 2. Generally compatible

| Medicine | Notes |
|---|---|
| Paracetamol | First choice for pain and fever |
| Ibuprofen (short-term) | Preferred NSAID while breastfeeding |
| Penicillins (for example amoxicillin, flucloxacillin) | Watch the baby for diarrhoea or thrush |
| Cephalosporins (for example cephalexin) | As above |
| Most insulins | Do not pass into milk in meaningful amounts |

## 3. Use with caution or avoid

| Drug | Lactation |
|---|---|
| Codeine (and tramadol) | Avoid — infant CNS depression (ultra-rapid metabolisers) |
| Metronidazole | Short courses OK; pause breastfeeding 12–24 h after a single high dose |
| Fluoroquinolones (for example ciprofloxacin) | Avoid if alternatives exist |
| Bromocriptine | Suppresses lactation |
| Chemotherapy (cytotoxic medicines) | Generally contraindicated; specialist advice |
| Radiopharmaceuticals | Temporary interruption as instructed by the nuclear medicine team |
| Estrogen-containing contraceptives | May reduce milk supply; progestogen-only methods usually preferred |
| Pseudoephedrine decongestants | May reduce milk supply; avoid regular use |

Codeine is converted to morphine in the body. In mothers who are "ultra-rapid metabolisers", high morphine levels can reach the baby and cause drowsiness, poor feeding and breathing problems. Because we cannot know who is at risk, codeine is avoided while breastfeeding.

## 4. Mastitis

Mastitis is painful inflammation of the breast, sometimes with infection. The mother should **continue breastfeeding** or expressing from the affected side; this helps recovery and the milk is safe for the baby. When the doctor prescribes an antibiotic, **flucloxacillin or cephalexin** are compatible choices. Paracetamol or ibuprofen help pain and fever, along with rest, fluids and gentle warm compresses before feeds.

Refer to a doctor if she has fever and flu-like symptoms, a hard red area that is spreading or not improving within about a day, pus or a painful lump (possible abscess), or if she feels very unwell. Do not supply an antibiotic without a prescription; follow the current Egyptian Drug Authority and Ministry of Health rules and the Lotus SOP.

## 5. Timing doses and watching the baby

For a medicine that needs care, advise taking the dose **right after a feed**, or before the baby's longest sleep, so the level in the milk is lower at the next feed. Prefer single-ingredient products, the lowest effective dose and the shortest course. Ask the mother to watch the baby for unusual sleepiness, poor feeding, irritability, rash or diarrhoea, and to seek medical advice if these appear.

**Counselling (Arabic):** "معظم المضادات البنسلين آمنة في الرضاعة. الكوديين ممكن يسبب نعاس للرضيع — تجنبيه. استمري في الرضاعة مع التهاب الثدي مع المضاد المناسب."

## 6. Privacy and sensitivity

Breastfeeding mothers are often tired and anxious and may come with a crying baby or send a relative. Offer a seat and a quieter spot, keep breast-related questions discreet, and never criticise a mother's feeding choice. Confirm the baby's age and whether the baby was premature or has any illness, because these change the advice.

## Scenario

> A breastfeeding mother, a few days after a caesarean section, brings a prescription that includes a codeine-containing painkiller.

Model approach: The pharmacist recognises that codeine should be avoided while breastfeeding because of the risk of drowsiness and breathing problems in the baby. The pharmacist contacts the prescriber to suggest alternative analgesia, such as regular paracetamol with ibuprofen if suitable for her, and explains to the mother that these are compatible with breastfeeding. The pharmacist encourages her to keep breastfeeding, explains the warning signs in the baby, and advises her to contact the doctor if her pain is not controlled or her wound becomes red, hot or discharging.

## Key takeaways

- Penicillins are compatible with breastfeeding.
- Avoid codeine while breastfeeding.
- With mastitis, continue breastfeeding with an appropriate antibiotic when prescribed.
- Avoid fluoroquinolones if alternatives exist.
- LactMed is the reference standard for lactation.
- Time doses of higher-risk medicines straight after a feed.
- Stopping breastfeeding is rarely necessary; check before advising it.
