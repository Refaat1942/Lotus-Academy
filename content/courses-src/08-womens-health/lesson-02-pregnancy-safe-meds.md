## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Apply a structured pregnancy risk assessment before dispensing or recommending any medicine
2. List medicines generally considered acceptable in pregnancy, such as paracetamol and many antibiotics
3. Identify medicines that are contraindicated or must be avoided, such as ACE inhibitors, valproate and warfarin
4. Counsel on options for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy and recognise when to refer
5. Use the FDA Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR) in context, including why letter categories are no longer relied on

---

## 1. Pregnancy risk assessment: always ask first

Many women do not mention pregnancy unless asked, and in the first weeks some do not yet know. Medicines can affect the baby at any stage, but the highest risk of structural birth defects is in the first trimester, while some medicines cause harm mainly later in pregnancy. On the other hand, untreated illness (for example high fever, uncontrolled epilepsy, high blood pressure or diabetes) can also harm the mother and baby. The aim is not to avoid all medicines but to use the safest effective option.

A simple routine at the counter:

1. **Ask**: "Are you pregnant, could you be pregnant, or are you planning a pregnancy?" Ask in private and in a respectful tone.
2. **Establish the stage**: how many weeks or which trimester.
3. **Check the medicine**: the leaflet, an up-to-date reference and the current Lotus guidance.
4. **Prefer the simplest, best-established option** at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time.
5. **Contact the prescriber** whenever a prescribed medicine looks unsuitable for pregnancy, and refer when symptoms need assessment.

## 2. From letter categories to PLLR

Older references and some leaflets in Egypt still show the FDA letter categories (A, B, C, D, X). The FDA replaced these letters with the **Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR)**, which uses written sections on pregnancy, lactation and females and males of reproductive potential. The narrative explains what the evidence shows, the size of any risk and the clinical considerations. The letters were often misread as a simple grading, so use them only as a rough flag and read the full information. When in doubt, ask the prescriber.

## 3. Generally considered acceptable

| Medicine | Notes |
|---|---|
| Paracetamol | First choice for pain and fever; lowest effective dose, shortest time |
| Many penicillins and cephalosporins | Generally considered safe when an antibiotic is prescribed |
| Insulin | Standard treatment for diabetes in pregnancy |
| Some antihistamines (for example loratadine) | Often used when an antihistamine is needed; check the reference |

"Generally safe" does not mean every product containing that ingredient is suitable. Combination cold and flu products may contain decongestants or other ingredients that should be avoided, so check every ingredient.

## 4. Contraindicated or to be avoided

| Drug class | Risk |
|---|---|
| ACE-I/ARB (for example lisinopril, losartan) | Fetal renal damage; avoid throughout pregnancy |
| Valproate | Neural tube defects and other developmental harm |
| Warfarin | Teratogenic in the first trimester; bleeding risk later |
| Methotrexate | Teratogenic; can cause pregnancy loss |
| Live vaccines | Fetal infection risk |
| NSAIDs (for example ibuprofen, diclofenac) | Third trimester: premature closure of the ductus arteriosus; avoid unless a doctor advises, especially in later pregnancy |
| Isotretinoin and oral retinoids | Severe birth defects; pregnancy must be excluded |
| Tetracyclines (for example doxycycline) | Tooth discolouration and bone effects; usually avoided |

If a pregnant woman is already taking one of these, she should **not stop a prescribed medicine on her own** where stopping could be dangerous (for example an antiepileptic). Contact the prescriber urgently.

## 5. Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy

Nausea is common, mainly in the first trimester. First advise small, frequent, dry meals, avoiding trigger smells and drinking fluids in small sips; ginger may help some women. Medicines used under medical advice include **pyridoxine** (vitamin B6), the **doxylamine-pyridoxine** combination (Diclegis; limited availability) and **metoclopramide** for short-term use when prescribed.

Refer the same day if she cannot keep fluids down, passes very little or dark urine, feels dizzy or faint, is losing weight or vomits blood. These can be signs of severe vomiting of pregnancy (hyperemesis) or dehydration.

**Counselling (Arabic):** "لو حامل، قولي للصيدلي قبل أي دواء. الباراسيتامول آمن. الإيبوبروفين ممنوع في آخر الحمل. ممنوع أدوية الضغط من مجموعة ACE."

## 6. Red flags in pregnancy

Refer urgently to a doctor or emergency service for vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, severe headache with visual disturbance or sudden swelling of the face and hands, fever, leaking of fluid, reduced baby movements later in pregnancy, or burning urination with fever or back pain. Never offer an OTC product to "wait and see" for these symptoms.

## 7. Privacy and sensitivity

Pregnancy may not yet be announced to the family, and some women ask about medicines after a loss. Ask about pregnancy quietly, do not announce it to others, and accept a family member speaking on her behalf while still asking for the information you need. Avoid frightening language: say "this one is not suitable in pregnancy; let us find a safer option with your doctor".

## Scenario

> A pregnant woman brings a new prescription for lisinopril for high blood pressure.

Model approach: The pharmacist recognises that lisinopril is an ACE inhibitor and is contraindicated in pregnancy because of the risk of fetal kidney damage. The pharmacist explains calmly, without alarming her, that the medicine needs to be reviewed and contacts the prescriber urgently, the same day, to discuss a safer antihypertensive such as methyldopa, labetalol or nifedipine. Raised blood pressure in pregnancy needs close medical follow-up, so the pharmacist makes sure she is not left without a plan and reminds her of the warning symptoms (severe headache, visual changes, sudden swelling).

## Key takeaways

- Paracetamol is the first-line choice for pain in pregnancy, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time.
- ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in all trimesters.
- Valproate should never be used in pregnancy without specialist decision; never stop it abruptly.
- Avoid NSAIDs in the third trimester, and use them only on medical advice earlier in pregnancy.
- Penicillins are generally considered safe.
- Always check for pregnancy before dispensing, and use the PLLR narrative, not letter categories alone.
- Refer pregnancy red flags immediately.
