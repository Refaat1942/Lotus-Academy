## 1. Pigmentation basics

Skin colour comes from a pigment made by skin cells. When skin is stimulated, those cells can make more pigment in one area and a dark spot appears. This section is general skin-care knowledge.

Common situations that customers describe:

| Situation | Plain-language description |
|---|---|
| Marks after acne or small injuries | Dark marks that stay after a pimple or irritation has healed |
| Melasma | Patchy darker areas, often on the face, which can be linked to hormones and sun |
| Sun spots | Spots that appear on areas often exposed to the sun |
| Friction or darker folds | Darker skin on knees, elbows, underarms or between the thighs |
| Redness and irritation | Inflamed skin that may leave a dark mark afterwards |

Pharmacists do not diagnose. A flat, even spot that has not changed is a cosmetic concern. A spot that is **new, growing, raised, bleeding, itchy, with irregular edges or changing colour** needs a doctor's opinion before any product is used.

## 2. How brightening routines work

In simple words, brightening products aim to reduce the appearance of extra pigment gradually. Three ideas help the customer understand:

1. **It is gradual.** Skin renews itself slowly. Visible change usually takes weeks, and deeper marks can take longer. Promising results in a few days damages trust.
2. **It is a routine, not a single cream.** Gentle cleansing, a brightening product at the recommended time, a moisturiser and daily sun protection work together.
3. **Sun undoes the work.** Sun exposure stimulates pigment production. Without daily sun protection, spots can darken again and the routine seems to fail.

### Why daily sun protection is essential

- Egypt has strong sun almost all year, even on cloudy days and in the car by the window.
- A brightening routine without sunscreen is the most common reason for disappointing results.
- The ISIS list includes Uveblock invisible (SPF 80, cream) and Uveblock fluid (SPF 50+, fluid). Customers who dislike a heavy feel may prefer the fluid; this is general guidance for conversation.
- Remind customers that sunscreen is applied every morning, not only at the beach.

### Patience and honesty

> "Brightening takes weeks of regular use. If you stop early or skip sun protection, the spots may come back. If the skin becomes red, burning or peeling, stop and ask us or your doctor."

## 5. Safe counselling checklist

1. **Ask first:** where are the spots, how long have they been there, are they changing, is the skin irritated?
2. **Pregnancy or breastfeeding:** do not decide yourself. Ask her to consult her doctor and read the leaflet.
3. **Patch test** on a small area before the first full application, and wait for the skin to respond.
4. **Apply only to the intended area** and avoid broken, burned or very irritated skin.
5. **Daily sunscreen** every morning, and a hat or shade when possible.
6. **Sensitive areas:** only the product the sheet lists for sensitive areas, as directed in the leaflet.
7. **Watch for reactions:** persistent burning, strong redness, swelling or blistering means stop and see a doctor.
8. **Time limit:** no improvement after a reasonable trial or long use without advice means a visit to a dermatologist, not a stronger product.
9. **Vitiligo or white patches:** this is not a brightening matter. See a dermatologist.

### What not to say

- "This will remove all your spots in one week."
- "You do not need sunscreen with this cream."
- "It is natural so it is safe for everyone."
- "Use more cream to be faster."

## 6. Referral triggers

Refer to a dermatologist when:

- a spot is new, growing, raised, bleeding or changing colour or shape
- pigmentation appears suddenly or spreads quickly
- the customer has had no improvement after a reasonable trial
- there is irritation, burning, swelling or blistering
- the customer is pregnant or breastfeeding and wants a treatment product, so the doctor decides
- the customer has a medical skin condition or takes medicines that affect the skin

## Scenario

A customer in her thirties comes to a Lotus branch asking for "something to remove the dark patches on my cheeks". They appeared after her pregnancy and she is still breastfeeding. The pharmacist asks about the patches and about breastfeeding, explains that patches like these may be linked to hormones and sun, and that she should ask her doctor before choosing a treatment product. Meanwhile the pharmacist recommends daily sun protection and gentle care, and explains that any brightening routine will take weeks. The customer agrees to see her doctor and returns later with the doctor's advice. The pharmacist then reads the leaflet of the chosen product with her, explains a patch test, and reminds her about sunscreen every morning. At no point does the pharmacist promise a fast result.

## Key takeaways

- Brightening is gradual, so set expectations in weeks and never in days
- Daily sun protection is the most important part of any brightening routine in Egypt
- ISIS: Unitone cream for general brightening and red skin, Unitone 4 (30 ml) for spots, melasma and dark knees and elbows, Unitone 4 Advanced and Neotone for night use, Unitone 4 with Arbuten for sensitive areas
- A.C.M.: Depiwhite advanced once in the evening on the spots; Depiwhite SPF50 day cream, used as the leaflet states
- The sheet's active ingredient wording needs checking: tell customers to read the leaflet for the exact ingredient and strength
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding: ask the doctor and read the leaflet
- Refer changing spots, irritation, white patches and no-improvement cases to a dermatologist
