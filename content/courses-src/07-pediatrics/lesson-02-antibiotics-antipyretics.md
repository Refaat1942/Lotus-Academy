## 1. Pediatric antibiotics

Antibiotics are prescription-only medicines. Dispense them according to a valid prescription and the current rules of the Egyptian Drug Authority and the Lotus SOP. The pharmacist's job is to check the dose against the child's weight, choose the right strength, prepare the suspension correctly and counsel the parent.

| Drug | Brand | Dose example (check leaflet and prescriber) |
|---|---|---|
| Amoxicillin | Amoxil suspension | 25–45 mg/kg/day, divided into 2–3 doses |
| Amoxicillin-clavulanate | Augmentin ES | High dose: 90 mg/kg/day of the amoxicillin component, divided into 2 doses (45 mg/kg per dose), e.g. for otitis media |
| Cefalexin | Ceporex syrup | 25–50 mg/kg/day, divided |

Notice that antibiotic doses are usually written in **mg/kg/day**. Divide by the number of doses per day before converting to mL. Augmentin is available in several strengths and ratios (standard suspensions and the high-dose ES suspension); doses are calculated on the **amoxicillin component**, and one strength must never be substituted for another without recalculating and checking with the prescriber.

**Antibiotic counselling checklist:**

- Ask about **allergy** to penicillin or cephalosporins before dispensing. A history of rash, swelling or breathing difficulty with an antibiotic must be discussed with the prescriber.
- Reconstitute with boiled, cooled water to the mark; **shake well** before each dose; store as the leaflet says (some suspensions need the fridge) and discard after the stated number of days.
- Give at **regular intervals** and **complete the course** the doctor prescribed, even if the child improves.
- Mild loose stools can occur. Contact the doctor if there is severe or bloody diarrhoea, a rash, swelling or breathing difficulty.
- **Antibiotics only for bacterial indications.** Most colds, coughs and sore throats in children are viral, and antibiotics do not help them.

## 2. Antipyretics

| Medicine | Example brands | Usual pediatric dose | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paracetamol | Cetal drops and syrup | 10–15 mg/kg per dose every 4–6 hours | Maximum 4 doses in 24 hours; check all products for hidden paracetamol |
| Ibuprofen | Brufen pediatric syrup | 5–10 mg/kg per dose, usually every 6–8 hours | Typically from 6 months of age; give with food or milk; avoid if the child is dehydrated or vomiting a lot; follow the leaflet maximum |

**NEVER give aspirin to children and teenagers under 16** unless a specialist prescribes it for a specific condition. Aspirin is linked to **Reye syndrome**, a rare but serious illness affecting the liver and brain, especially after viral infections such as flu or chickenpox.

Common errors to prevent: giving paracetamol drops as if they were syrup, giving two products that both contain paracetamol, waking a sleeping child just to give a dose, and repeating doses too close together.

## 3. Fever is not always an emergency

Fever is the body's natural response to infection. The aim of an antipyretic is to make the child **comfortable**, not to bring the thermometer to normal at any cost. A child who is drinking, playing and alert with a moderate fever can often be observed, given fluids and dressed lightly.

**Counselling (Arabic):** "الحرارة مش عدو — جسم الطفل بيقاتل العدو. باراسيتامول لو حرارة تعبيه أو فوق 38.5. ممنوع أسبرين للأطفال."

*English meaning:* "Fever is not the enemy; the child's body is fighting the infection. Give paracetamol if the fever is distressing him or is above 38.5. Aspirin is forbidden for children." Emphasise that the child's comfort matters more than the exact number.

**Fever red flags (refer to a doctor or emergency care):**

- Any fever in a baby **under 3 months**
- The child is very drowsy, floppy, hard to wake or inconsolable
- Difficulty breathing, a rash that does not fade when pressed, a stiff neck, or severe headache
- Signs of dehydration: very few wet nappies, no tears, dry mouth
- A seizure
- Fever that lasts more than about two days, or keeps returning, or a child with a chronic illness

## 4. Febrile seizures

Some young children, usually between about 6 months and 5 years, have a seizure when their temperature rises. It is very frightening for parents but usually brief. Counsel:

- Stay calm, place the child **on the side** on a safe surface, and remove nearby hard objects.
- **Do not put anything in the mouth** and do not try to restrain the movements.
- Note the time. Call emergency services if it lasts **more than 5 minutes**, if breathing is difficult, or if the child does not recover quickly.
- Any **first seizure** needs medical assessment.
- Antipyretics make the child more comfortable but **do not prevent** febrile seizures, so parents should not give extra doses for this reason.

## Scenario

> A mother brings an 8-month-old with a temperature of 38.2°C since this morning. The baby is active, playing and feeding well. She wants "an antibiotic and a strong fever medicine".

**Model approach:** The pharmacist asks about age, weight, duration, feeding, wet nappies, breathing, rash and drowsiness. With no red flags, the pharmacist reassures her that fever is the body fighting infection, recommends observation, frequent feeds and fluids, light clothing, and paracetamol by weight **only if the baby is distressed**. The pharmacist explains that antibiotics need a doctor's decision and usually do not help viral fevers, writes the dose in mL on the box, and lists the red flags that mean the baby must see a doctor.

## Key takeaways

- Augmentin ES is a high-dose suspension, e.g. for otitis media; doses are calculated on the amoxicillin component
- Paracetamol 10–15 mg/kg per dose, maximum 4 doses in 24 hours
- Ibuprofen typically from 6 months only, with food, and not in dehydration
- No aspirin for children under 16 because of Reye syndrome
- Fever does not always need treatment; treat distress and watch for red flags
- Antibiotics are only for bacterial indications decided by a doctor
