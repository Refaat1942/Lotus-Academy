## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Counsel on **vitamin D 400 IU/day** for infants
2. Identify **Vidrop, Fer-In-Sol and Maltofer** and their correct use
3. Counsel on **iron staining of teeth**, giving liquid iron with a syringe towards the back of the cheek
4. Recognise **poor growth (failure to thrive)** and refer
5. Warn about **iron overdose toxicity** and safe storage

## 1. Vitamin D

Vitamin D is needed for strong bones and teeth. Breast milk contains little vitamin D, so **breastfed infants are commonly advised to take 400 IU of vitamin D per day**, starting in the first days of life, as directed by the pediatrician. Infant formula already contains vitamin D, so a formula-fed baby's need for drops should be decided by the doctor.

**Vidrop** drops are a common vitamin D product in Egypt. Counsel parents to give the dose stated by the doctor or the leaflet, using the product's own dropper, once a day at a fixed time. Avoid giving two vitamin D products at the same time, for example drops plus a multivitamin that also contains vitamin D, unless the doctor has said so, because too much vitamin D can be harmful.

Long-term deficiency can cause **rickets**: soft, weak bones, bowed legs, delayed walking or teething, and poor growth. A child with any of these signs should be **referred to a doctor**; supplements from the shelf are not a substitute for assessment.

## 2. Iron

Iron deficiency is a common cause of anaemia in young children and can affect energy, appetite and development. Iron treatment should follow a doctor's assessment and dose, because the right amount depends on the child's weight and blood results.

Common products include **Fer-In-Sol** (ferrous sulfate drops) and **Maltofer** (iron polymaltose). Counsel:

- Give iron **away from milk, dairy and calcium by about 2 hours**, and away from tea. Juice containing vitamin C can help absorption.
- **Darkening of the stool is normal** on iron and is not a reason to stop. Stomach upset or constipation can occur; tell the doctor if it is troublesome.
- **Liquid iron can stain the teeth.** Give it with an oral syringe **towards the back of the cheek** (inside the mouth, not onto the front teeth), then give a drink of water or brush the teeth. Staining is usually temporary and can be brushed away.
- Large amounts of cow's milk in toddlers can reduce appetite for iron-rich foods. Encourage a varied diet and refer dietary questions to the pediatrician.

## 3. Multivitamins

Pediatric multivitamin products (the original course mentions examples such as Bebelac vitamins and Centrum kids) are a **supplement only if the diet is inadequate**. They do not replace a balanced diet and should be chosen for the child's age. Chewables and gummies look like sweets: store them out of reach and never call them "candy".

**Counselling (Arabic):** "فيتامين د يومياً للرضع. الحديد يتاخد بعيد عن الحليب. احفظ الحديد بعيد عن الأطفال — سم لو أخدوا كتير."

*English meaning:* "Vitamin D daily for infants. Take iron away from milk. Keep iron away from children; it is poison if they take a lot."

## 4. Iron overdose: an emergency

Iron is one of the most dangerous medicines for a small child to swallow by accident. A few adult iron tablets, especially those of a pregnant mother, can seriously poison a toddler. Early symptoms may include vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal pain, and a child may seem to improve before becoming more seriously ill.

- **Store iron in child-resistant containers**, high and locked away, and never leave tablets on a table or in a handbag.
- If a child may have swallowed iron, this is an **emergency**: go to the nearest emergency department or contact the poison centre immediately, even if the child seems well. **Do not induce vomiting** at home. Bring the pack so the amount can be estimated. Hospitals may give specific treatment such as deferoxamine.

## 5. Growth and failure to thrive

Parents often ask for "a vitamin to make my child eat" or "something to make him gain weight". Ask about feeding, illness, stools, development and growth records. **Failure to thrive** means a child is not gaining weight or growing as expected. Signs include falling weight on the growth chart, poor feeding, frequent illness, or delayed development. These children **must be referred** to a pediatrician; appetite products or vitamins can delay a diagnosis.

## Scenario

> A mother calls the branch in panic: her 2-year-old found her iron tablets in a handbag and swallowed "some". The child has vomited once.

**Model approach:** The pharmacist treats this as an **emergency**. The pharmacist tells the mother to go to the nearest emergency department or contact the poison centre **now**, not to wait for more symptoms, not to make the child vomit, and to bring the tablet pack. The pharmacist explains that hospitals may need to give specific treatment such as deferoxamine. After the emergency, the pharmacist reinforces storage in child-resistant containers out of reach.

## Key takeaways

- Vitamin D 400 IU/day is commonly advised for breastfed infants, as directed by the doctor
- Vidrop is a common vitamin D drop in Egypt; avoid doubling vitamin D products
- Give iron away from milk and dairy by about 2 hours
- Dark stools are normal on iron; give liquid iron towards the back of the cheek to limit staining
- Iron overdose is an emergency; never induce vomiting at home
- Store iron and vitamins in child-resistant packs out of reach
- Poor growth or failure to thrive needs a physician, not more vitamins
