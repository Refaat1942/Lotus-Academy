## Learning objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

1. Calculate **mg/kg dosing** and convert a dose in milligrams into a volume in millilitres
2. Select **syrup, drops, dispersible** and suppository forms appropriately
3. Identify **Egyptian pediatric brands** commonly seen at the counter and check their strength
4. Counsel **accurate measurement** using the right device
5. Avoid **adult formulations** in children

---

## 1. Dosing principles

Pediatric dosing is **always weight-based**. A child's ability to absorb, distribute, metabolise and eliminate a medicine changes with growth, so the safe dose is calculated per kilogram of body weight (mg/kg), following the product leaflet, the prescriber and recognised pediatric references. Old rules that scale an adult dose by age or body size, such as **Clark's rule or Young's rule, are outdated** and should not be used at the counter.

Age bands printed on boxes ("2–6 years: 5 mL") are **approximate only**. Two children of the same age can differ in weight a lot, so when an age band and a weight-based dose disagree, the weight-based dose and the prescriber's instruction come first.

**The three-step calculation:**

1. **Weigh or confirm the weight.** Ask for a recent weight in kilograms. If the parent is unsure, weigh the child on the pharmacy scale if available. Never guess the weight from age.
2. **Dose in mg = dose in mg/kg × weight in kg.** Check whether the reference dose is *per dose* or *per day*. A "per day" dose must be divided by the number of doses.
3. **Volume in mL = dose in mg ÷ concentration in mg/mL.** A syrup labelled 120 mg/5 mL contains 24 mg in each mL.

Then check that the result does not exceed the maximum single dose or the maximum daily dose stated on the leaflet, and never exceeds the adult dose.

*Worked example (training only):* paracetamol is commonly dosed at 10–15 mg/kg per dose. For a 10 kg child that is 100–150 mg. With a 120 mg/5 mL syrup, 5 mL gives 120 mg, which is 12 mg/kg and within the range. With a 250 mg/5 mL syrup, the same 120 mg is only about 2.4 mL. The same "5 mL" of the stronger syrup would be 250 mg, which is too much. This is why the **concentration must always be read aloud** with the parent.

## 2. Choosing the formulation

| Formulation | Use |
|---|---|
| Syrup or suspension (strength varies, e.g. 120 mg/5 mL or 250 mg/5 mL) | Paracetamol, antibiotics and many other medicines |
| Drops (concentrated, with their own dropper) | Vitamin D, some antipyretics and some antibiotics for infants |
| Dispersible tablets | Zinc, some antibiotics; dissolved in a little water |
| Suppositories | When the child is vomiting or cannot take oral medicine; young infants |

Practical points:

- **Suspensions** (most antibiotic powders) are reconstituted with the exact amount of boiled, cooled water to the mark, shaken well before every dose, stored as the leaflet says, and discarded after the number of days stated on the leaflet.
- **Drops are much more concentrated** than syrup. Never give a "drops" volume as if it were syrup, or the reverse.
- **Suppositories** are useful during vomiting, but the dose is still weight-based and the parent must not give oral and rectal paracetamol together.
- **Avoid adult formulations.** Crushing or splitting adult tablets gives inaccurate doses, and adult products may contain strengths or ingredients not suited to children. Pediatric formulations are safer.

## 3. Egyptian products at the counter

Common pediatric products you will see include **Augmentin suspension** (amoxicillin-clavulanate), **Ceporex syrup** (cefalexin), **Vidrop** (vitamin D drops) and **Cetal pediatric drops** (paracetamol drops). Many brands come in more than one strength (drops, syrup, "forte" or "ES" versions). Before dispensing, confirm the active ingredient, the strength and the form against the prescription, and point to the strength on the box when counselling.

## 4. Measuring accurately

- The **oral syringe is the most accurate** device, especially for volumes under 5 mL. Give one with every liquid if the pack does not contain one.
- Use **the device that comes with the product**. A dropper is calibrated for its own bottle only; do not swap droppers between products.
- **Never use kitchen spoons.** Tea and table spoons vary widely in size.
- Show the parent the exact mark on the syringe, and write the dose in mL and the times on the box.

**Counselling (Arabic):** "استخدم السرنجة المرفقة — مش ملعقة الأكل. الجرعة حسب الوزن مش العمر بس."

*English meaning:* "Use the syringe provided, not a kitchen spoon. The dose depends on weight, not only on age."

## 5. When to refer

Refer to a doctor rather than self-treat when the child is under 3 months and unwell, very drowsy or hard to wake, not feeding or drinking, has difficulty breathing, has signs of dehydration, or when the parent cannot give a weight and the medicine has a narrow margin. If an overdose may have happened, send the family to emergency care immediately with the pack.

## Scenario

> A mother says she has been giving her 10 kg child half of an adult 500 mg paracetamol tablet, crushed in juice, for fever.

**Model approach:** The pharmacist stays calm and asks how many doses have been given in the last 24 hours. Half a tablet is 250 mg, which is about 25 mg/kg for this child: well above the usual 10–15 mg/kg per dose. If several such doses have been given in 24 hours, or the child seems unwell, the pharmacist advises urgent medical assessment and does not wait for symptoms. If only one dose was given and the child is well, the pharmacist explains the risk, recommends a pediatric paracetamol syrup, shows the strength on the box, calculates the dose by weight (for example 5 mL of 120 mg/5 mL), demonstrates the syringe, explains the minimum interval between doses and the maximum number of doses per day from the leaflet, and advises seeing a doctor if the fever lasts or red flags appear.

## Key takeaways

- Weight-based dosing (mg/kg) is mandatory; age bands are approximate only
- Check whether a dose is per dose or per day, and always read the concentration
- The oral syringe is the most accurate measuring device; never use kitchen spoons
- Drops are concentrated and must be measured with their own calibrated dropper
- Vidrop is a standardised vitamin D drop product; give it with its own dropper as the leaflet directs
- Pediatric formulations are safer than crushed adult tablets
- Follow the leaflet and the prescriber, and refer when red flags appear
