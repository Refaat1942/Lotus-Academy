## 1. Oral rehydration solution (ORS)

In children with diarrhoea or vomiting, the main danger is **dehydration**, not the diarrhoea itself. ORS is the cornerstone of treatment. Use the WHO-formula (low-osmolarity) ORS sachets available in the pharmacy, and reconstitute correctly:

- Wash hands and use clean drinking water (boiled and cooled for infants).
- Dissolve **one whole sachet in the exact volume of water written on the sachet**. Sachet sizes differ: some are made up to 1 litre, others to a smaller volume such as 200 mL. Read the pack with the parent.
- **Do not add sugar, salt, juice or milk**, and do not make "half sachets". The wrong concentration can make diarrhoea worse or be harmful.
- Give **small, frequent sips** with a spoon, cup or syringe. If the child vomits, wait about 10 minutes and continue more slowly.
- **Continue breastfeeding** and normal feeding as tolerated.
- Keep prepared solution covered and discard it after the time stated on the pack (usually 24 hours).

**Zinc** reduces the duration and severity of diarrhoea in children. The WHO dose is **10 mg/day for infants under 6 months and 20 mg/day for children over 6 months, for 10–14 days**, even after the diarrhoea stops. Dispersible zinc tablets or zinc syrups are used; follow the product leaflet.

## 2. Recognising dehydration and red flags

| Level | Signs | Action |
|---|---|---|
| No or mild dehydration | Alert, drinking normally, wet nappies | ORS and zinc at home, continue feeding |
| Some dehydration | Thirsty, restless, dry mouth, fewer wet nappies, few tears | ORS carefully; the child should be assessed by a doctor |
| Severe dehydration | **Lethargic or hard to wake**, sunken eyes, unable to drink, very little urine | **Urgent care immediately**; home ORS alone is insufficient |

Other red flags: blood in the stool, green (bile-stained) or persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, a swollen abdomen, high fever, a young infant with diarrhoea, or diarrhoea that does not improve after a few days.

## 3. Medicines: what to use and what to avoid

- **Loperamide: avoid in young children.** It can cause serious side effects in children and may hide a worsening illness. Do not supply it for children unless a doctor specifically prescribes it.
- **Antiemetics are prescription decisions.** Ondansetron may be prescribed by a doctor for severe vomiting. Domperidone, if prescribed, should be a short course at a weight-based dose per the physician. Do not supply antiemetics for children without a prescription.
- **Probiotics** such as **Enterogermina** (Bacillus clausii) and other pediatric probiotic products such as Lacinox can be used as an **adjunct**, for example to help prevent antibiotic-associated diarrhoea (AAD). They support but never replace ORS and zinc.
- Antibiotics are not routinely needed for children's diarrhoea; that is a doctor's decision.

## 4. Infant colic

Colic is long periods of intense crying in a healthy, well-fed baby, often in the late afternoon or evening. It usually starts in the first weeks, **peaks around 6 weeks** and **improves by about 3–4 months**.

**Non-drug measures first:** holding and rocking, safe swaddling, gentle white noise or a car or pram ride, checking feeding technique and burping, a calm routine, and **parental support**. Parents are often exhausted; encourage them to take turns and to put the baby down safely and step away for a few minutes if they feel overwhelmed. **Never shake a baby.**

**Products:** simethicone (for example Infacol) is widely used but its benefit is **limited**. Some probiotics may help selected breastfed babies. **Dicyclomine must not be used in infants.**

**Refer if the crying baby** has fever, vomiting (especially green), poor feeding or poor weight gain, blood in the stool, a weak or high-pitched cry, unusual drowsiness, or if the parent is very worried or not coping.

**Counselling (Arabic):** "محلول الجفاف يتحل في كمية المية المكتوبة على الكيس بالظبط — متزودش سكر. الزنك يقصر مدة الإسهال. المغص يتحسن غالباً بعد 3 شهور."

*English meaning:* "Dissolve the rehydration solution in exactly the amount of water written on the sachet; do not add sugar. Zinc shortens diarrhoea. Colic usually improves after 3 months."

## Scenario

> A father arrives with a 2-year-old who has had diarrhoea and vomiting since yesterday. The child is lethargic, hard to keep awake and has not passed urine since the morning. He asks for "something to stop the diarrhoea".

**Model approach:** The pharmacist recognises signs of **severe dehydration** and explains clearly that the child needs **urgent medical care now**. The pharmacist does not supply loperamide or an antiemetic. ORS can be started with small sips **while the family is on the way** to the emergency department or doctor, but the father is told that home treatment alone is **not enough** in this situation. The pharmacist stays calm, gives simple instructions and does not delay the family.

## Key takeaways

- ORS is the cornerstone of treating diarrhoea and dehydration; prepare it with the exact water volume on the sachet
- Zinc for 10–14 days in diarrhoea (10 mg under 6 months, 20 mg over 6 months)
- No loperamide in young children; antiemetics only on prescription
- Colic peaks around 6 weeks and usually improves by 3–4 months; support the parents
- Probiotics are an adjunct, for example in antibiotic-associated diarrhoea
- Lethargy or severe dehydration needs urgent care; home ORS alone is insufficient
